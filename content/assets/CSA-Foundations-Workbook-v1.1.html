Section 1

Orientation — Why This Matters

Why is sleep and airway a Chiropractic problem?

Above–Down, Inside–Out (ADIO) has been a foundational principle of Chiropractic for nearly 130 years. In everyday clinical practice, however, ADIO has most often been applied from the spine down, with pain serving as the primary point of entry. This workbook proposes an expanded framework — ADIO from the skull down — beginning with sleep and airway.

This perspective moves Chiropractic beyond a narrow musculoskeletal focus and toward a function-centered model that prioritizes how the body adapts, regulates, and restores itself. Using the Triad of Health concept, this approach begins with sleep and airway as expressions of neurological and rhythmic regulation, then considers inflammation and biochemistry, and finally addresses structural alignment.

By starting with sleep and airway, clinical evaluation shifts toward the conditions that allow the nervous system and body to recover, adapt, and coordinate function.

This workbook is designed to provide orientation to sleep and airway as structural issues, creating awareness of patterns many Doctors of Chiropractic already observe but have not yet named.

Founder's Note

Why CSA Exists

CSA did not begin as a business idea. It began as a pattern I could not ignore.

Early in practice, patients began reporting something unexpected — their sleep improved after their first structural adjustment. Not after weeks of care. Not after protocols. After a single structural intervention.

At first, I dismissed it as coincidence. But the feedback persisted. That feedback led me into the sleep world — conferences, conversations, and clinical environments outside traditional Chiropractic spaces. What I found was not bad medicine, but incomplete thinking. Sleep was being measured, managed, and medicated, yet the full-body biomechanics that support airway stability were largely absent from the conversation.

At the same time, I noticed something else: when Chiropractors stepped into sleep, the value of their work changed. Not because sleep was "hot," but because it was important — and under-claimed. In a field still being defined, pioneers were setting both standards and prices.

CSA exists to do two things at once

Elevate the Doctor of Chiropractic into a clinically legitimate role in sleep and airway, and address one of the most serious, under-treated problems in modern health. This Academy is not about techniques. It is about responsibility — and the structural thinking required to carry it.

What this workbook is designed to do

It supports three shifts that many Doctors of Chiropractic sense, but have not yet been given language for:

A mental reframe — understanding sleep not as a collection of symptoms, but as a structural and biomechanical process.
A professional reframe — recognizing that Chiropractic belongs in the sleep and airway conversation, not as an adjunct, but as a structurally competent discipline.
A career reframe — acknowledging that this work matters, remains underclaimed, and requires a higher level of clinical responsibility.

Section 2

The Failure of the Current Model

The issue is not bad intentions — it is an incomplete model.

Sleep is largely treated as a medical problem rather than a structural one. Airway care is fragmented across MDs, dentists, ENTs, and sleep laboratories, with each discipline addressing a portion of the problem but rarely the whole patient. As a result, no single provider consistently evaluates how structure, posture, and biomechanics contribute to sleep dysfunction.

This fragmentation leads to symptom-first thinking. Snoring is treated as noise rather than collapse. Fatigue is approached as a chemical or psychological issue instead of a mechanical inefficiency. Apnea is reduced to an event count, while the structural failure that allows the airway to collapse is often left unexplored. Symptoms are managed, but the question of why the airway fails is frequently overlooked.

Within this model, equipment becomes the primary solution. CPAP is commonly positioned as a default rather than a last resort. For many patients CPAP is effective, first-line medicine; the concern here is not the device but the absence of a structural evaluation around it. Oral appliances may be prescribed without a global structural assessment. While these devices can manage airflow, they do not address the underlying structure, and long-term compliance challenges are often normalized rather than examined.

A major limitation of the current approach is the lack of meaningful structural evaluation. The skull is rarely assessed as a living, moving structure. The maxillae, palatine bones, and sphenoid are often ignored. Pelvic mechanics and posture are disconnected from airway thinking. Breathing is viewed as a pulmonary process rather than a biomechanical one involving the entire axial system.

Within this environment, Chiropractic is frequently marginalized. Doctors of Chiropractic are trained to adjust, but not consistently trained to evaluate airway as a structural system. Sleep is rarely emphasized in Chiropractic education, even though Chiropractors routinely encounter patients with sleep-related complaints. Many recognize the signs but lack a framework to name and organize what they are observing, allowing referral patterns to replace responsibility.

Finally, the current model suffers from measurement blind spots. Emphasis is placed on numerical metrics such as the Apnea–Hypopnea Index (AHI), while patient experience is underweighted. Daytime fatigue, brain fog, anxiety, and overall quality of life often receive less attention. Structural change is rarely tracked, even though it may be central to long-term improvement.

Section 3

Chiropractic's Missing Framework

Chiropractic has always emphasized the principle that structure governs function, yet airway has not been consistently integrated into this structural worldview. While Doctors of Chiropractic are trained to assess and influence biomechanics, airway is often approached as a localized issue rather than as a global structural system.

In many clinical settings, airway is reduced to the jaw, tongue, or soft tissues. The skull is treated as static rather than dynamic, and its relationship to cervical alignment, thoracic posture, and pelvic mechanics is rarely explored. Breathing is commonly framed in terms of respiratory chemistry, while the biomechanical coordination required to maintain airway patency is overlooked.

As a result, many Doctors of Chiropractic recognize airway-related patterns in their patients but lack a shared framework to organize those observations. Without a common language or model, clinical insights remain isolated, evaluation becomes inconsistent, and confidence in addressing airway-related issues is diminished.

What is missing is not intent or skill, but a coherent structural framework — one that connects airway, posture, and biomechanics into a unified clinical perspective.

Section 5

Clinical Thinking Shift

When airway and sleep are viewed through a structural lens, familiar clinical findings begin to take on new meaning. Snoring is no longer understood simply as noise, but as evidence of airway instability. Fatigue is reframed from a purely chemical or psychological issue to a sign of mechanical inefficiency. Apnea is seen not merely as an event count, but as a structural collapse occurring under physiological load.

This shift in perspective alters how clinical information is interpreted. Mouth breathing may be recognized as a compensatory pattern rather than a habit. Poor sleep becomes an indicator of reduced structural adaptability. Daytime symptoms are understood as reflections of nighttime biomechanics.

As these interpretations change, so does clinical curiosity. Patterns that once appeared unrelated begin to organize around structure, posture, and function. The need for a coherent framework becomes apparent — not to replace clinical judgment, but to support it.

Section 6

Case Reflection

Consider a patient commonly seen in Chiropractic practice. They may present with chronic fatigue, unrefreshing sleep, headaches, or neck and upper thoracic tension. Snoring may be reported but is often minimized. Sleep quality is discussed briefly, if at all, and attention is typically directed toward daytime symptoms.

Despite appropriate care, progress may plateau. Symptoms improve temporarily but return. The patient continues to report low energy, difficulty concentrating, or persistent discomfort. Structural findings are present, yet their relationship to sleep and airway is not fully explored.

When viewed through a structural airway lens, this familiar clinical picture takes on new meaning. What once appeared as a collection of unrelated complaints begins to organize around nighttime biomechanics and airway stability. The clinical question shifts from "What symptom should be addressed next?" to "What is failing to adapt during sleep?"

Section 7

What CSA Actually Is

CSA supports a shift in clinical mindset while providing a structural framework for evaluating sleep and airway.

The Chiropractic Sleep Academy is an educational framework designed to support Doctors of Chiropractic in developing a coherent understanding of sleep and airway through a structural lens. It is not a technique, device, or protocol, and it does not replace clinical judgment or professional responsibility.

CSA emphasizes foundational concepts before clinical application. Its purpose is to organize thinking, improve evaluation, and support clinical reasoning over time. Rather than offering isolated solutions, CSA provides an environment for ongoing learning, reflection, and refinement.

The Academy is intentionally designed as a continuum. As understanding deepens, clinical confidence grows — not through the accumulation of techniques, but through clarity of perspective. CSA exists to support Doctors of Chiropractic who recognize the importance of airway and sleep and who wish to approach these issues with structural coherence and professional integrity.

CSA is intentionally quiet. Its purpose is not to compete for attention, but to support clinical responsibility.
