# CSA Clinical Brief — Cortisol, Circadian Rhythm & the HPA Axis

**Audience:** Chiropractors training through Chiropractic Sleep Academy (CSA).
**Where it sits:** An extension of **Module 1 (Anatomy & Physiology).** Module 1
teaches the airway and the mechanics of sleep. This brief teaches the
**endocrine timing layer** underneath it — why *when* the body produces cortisol
is a readout of sleep and circadian integrity, and how that connects to the
structural airway case.

> **The scope line, stated once and meant throughout:**
> Chiropractors **do not diagnose** obstructive sleep apnea, and they **do not
> diagnose adrenal or endocrine disease.** A physician diagnoses. Your role is
> **structural** — assess, screen, educate, refer, and deliver structural care.
> Everything below exists to make you *fluent in the physiology*, not to make you
> an endocrinologist.

---

## 1. Why CSA cares about cortisol at all

CSA is a **sleep-first, skull-down** model. The airway is our structural lane.
But the reason a patient walks in — exhausted, foggy, waking at 3 a.m., wired at
night, flat in the morning — is rarely described in airway language. It's
described in **energy and timing** language.

Cortisol is the hormone that runs that timing. If you understand its rhythm, you
can:

1. **Hear** what the patient is actually telling you ("I'm dead until 11 a.m.
   and then I get a second wind at 10 p.m." is a rhythm complaint).
2. **Explain** why fragmented breathing at night shows up as a daytime symptom.
3. **Stay credible** in a room with dentists, MDs, and functional-medicine
   practitioners — and stay inside your lane while doing it.

This is a **teaching and communication** asset. It does not add a treatment to
your scope.

## 2. The rhythm — cortisol as a clock signal, not just a stress hormone

Cortisol is popularly framed as "the stress hormone." That framing is
incomplete. Cortisol is better understood as a **circadian signaling molecule**
that helps coordinate the body across the 24-hour day. Stress reactivity is one
of its functions, not its whole job.

**The normal 24-hour shape:**

- **Nadir (lowest point)** — around **midnight / the first hours of sleep.**
  This is when the axis should be quiet.
- **Pre-waking rise** — cortisol begins climbing in the **second half of the
  night**, several hours before the alarm. The body starts preparing for the day
  before you're conscious of it.
- **Cortisol Awakening Response (CAR)** — a further sharp rise in the **first
  ~30–45 minutes after waking.** This is a distinct, well-described phenomenon —
  not just the tail of the pre-waking climb.
- **Diurnal decline** — a steady, sustained fall across the day.
- **Back to nadir** — low again by late evening, allowing sleep and nighttime
  recovery.

**One nuance worth knowing:** cortisol is also **pulsatile** — released in
bursts roughly every 60–90 minutes riding on top of the daily curve. Receptor
signaling responds to that pulsing pattern, not just to the average level. This
is part of why a single random blood cortisol tells you very little about
rhythm.

**The clinical takeaway, in one sentence:**

> Cortisol regulation is not simply about *how much* is produced — it's about
> **when.**

A resilient HPA axis shows: an appropriately timed **morning rise**, a
**sustained daytime decline**, and **consistently low nocturnal cortisol.**

## 3. The mechanism — how the signal gets from the brain to the tissues

You do not need to teach this to a patient. You need to know it so you never
bluff.

**The axis:**

> **SCN** (suprachiasmatic nucleus — the master clock, entrained by light)
> → **PVN** of the hypothalamus → **CRH**
> → anterior pituitary → **ACTH**
> → adrenal cortex → **cortisol**
> → negative feedback back onto pituitary and hypothalamus

The SCN also reaches the adrenal gland through **autonomic innervation**, which
modulates how sensitive the adrenal is to ACTH at different times of day. So the
timing is driven from two directions, not one.

**Downstream — why the rhythm matters to every tissue:**

Cortisol crosses into peripheral tissues and binds **glucocorticoid receptors
(GR)**. Activated GR acts on **glucocorticoid response elements** in the
promoters of core clock genes (the *PER* family among them), which is how the
daily cortisol wave helps **entrain peripheral clocks** — liver, muscle, fat,
gut, immune cells — to the central clock in the brain.

A structurally important detail: the **SCN itself is largely GR-poor.** Cortisol
therefore synchronizes the *periphery* without feeding back to reset the master
clock. That asymmetry is exactly why a disrupted cortisol rhythm produces
**central-peripheral misalignment** — the brain's clock says one time, the
liver's clock says another.

**Receptor nuance:** cortisol binds both the high-affinity **mineralocorticoid
receptor (MR)** — largely occupied even at the trough — and the lower-affinity
**GR**, which fills as levels rise. This is another reason the *shape* of the
curve carries information that a single value cannot.

**What the rhythm helps coordinate:** nervous, endocrine, immune, metabolic, and
cardiovascular function — supporting daytime activity and permitting nighttime
recovery.

## 4. What flattens the curve

The classic disrupted pattern is a **flattened rhythm**: a **reduced morning
peak** and **elevated evening levels.** Less range, less signal.

Recognized contributors:

- **Sleep disruption and fragmentation** — including breathing-related arousals.
- **Chronic psychological stress.**
- **Nighttime light exposure** — the dominant input to the master clock is light;
  evening light is a timing signal in the wrong direction.
- **Shift work.**
- **Circadian misalignment generally** — irregular sleep/wake timing, social
  jetlag, late eating.

**The sleep physiology underneath it:** deep slow-wave sleep is associated with
**inhibition** of HPA activity, while **awakenings and arousals** are associated
with cortisol pulses. A night broken into pieces is a night in which the axis
keeps getting nudged when it should be quiet. Experimental sleep restriction has
been shown to raise **evening** cortisol — the exact end of the curve that
should be lowest.

## 5. The airway connection — stated honestly

Here is where a CSA doctor must be careful, because this is where it would be
easy to overreach.

**What is well supported:**
- Obstructive events produce **arousals** and **intermittent hypoxia.**
- Arousals and intermittent hypoxia produce **sympathetic activation** and
  fragment sleep architecture, including slow-wave sleep.
- Fragmented sleep and circadian disruption are associated with **altered
  HPA-axis activity** and a flatter diurnal cortisol pattern.

**What is *not* settled — say this out loud:**
- The literature on cortisol levels in **untreated OSA** is **heterogeneous**.
  Studies disagree; effects are modest and inconsistent.
- Studies of **CPAP therapy and cortisol** likewise show mixed and generally
  small effects.
- There is **no established cortisol signature that identifies sleep apnea**, and
  no cortisol test that substitutes for a home sleep test.

**Therefore the CSA position is:**

> Sleep-disordered breathing is one plausible and physiologically coherent
> contributor to circadian and HPA disruption — **alongside** light exposure,
> schedule, stress load, and behavior. We treat the airway and the structure
> because that's our lane and because breathing at night matters. We do **not**
> claim to "fix cortisol," and we do **not** use cortisol as a diagnostic for
> apnea.

That restraint is the credibility. Anyone can overstate this; a clinician who
states the uncertainty accurately is the one dentists and physicians will keep
in the room.

## 6. Language we do not use

**"Adrenal fatigue."** CSA does not use this term. It is not a recognized
diagnosis, systematic review has not supported it as an entity, and using it
puts a doctor immediately outside the credible conversation. It is also the
single fastest way for a chiropractor to be dismissed by an MD.

Say instead: **"circadian and HPA-axis rhythm disruption"**, or plainly, **"your
cortisol timing looks shifted."** Same clinical concern, defensible language.

**Also off-limits (CLAUDE.md §5):**
- Claiming structural care, VAET, or any product **corrects, resets, or cures**
  a cortisol rhythm or an endocrine condition.
- Implying the DC diagnoses OSA, Cushing's, adrenal insufficiency, or any
  endocrine disease.
- Attacking CPAP, oral appliances, or sleep medicine.

## 7. Red flags — refer, don't interpret

Rhythm complaints and **endocrine disease** can look alike from the outside.
Route these out, promptly and without drama:

- Unexplained weight change, purple striae, proximal muscle weakness, moon
  facies, new-onset diabetes or hypertension → **Cushing's syndrome workup.**
- Profound fatigue with hypotension, salt craving, hyperpigmentation, nausea,
  weight loss, electrolyte disturbance → **adrenal insufficiency workup.**
- Anyone on **corticosteroid therapy** — exogenous steroids suppress the axis and
  make any rhythm interpretation meaningless.
- **Central apnea** or medical red flags on screening — refer, always (Module 1
  §7).

"I want you to have this looked at by your physician" is a strength move, not a
lost case.

## 8. What a CSA doctor actually does with this

Within scope, and without becoming a functional-medicine practice:

**1 — Take the timing history.** Add rhythm questions to the CSA consult:
- What time do you wake, on workdays and days off?
- Do you wake before your alarm, or does it drag you out?
- Are you dragging in the morning and wired at night?
- Screens, bright light, or work after 9 p.m.?
- Any shift work, rotating schedule, or overnight call?

This costs 60 seconds and it materially sharpens the case picture.

**2 — Connect it to the objective data.** The HST is still the anchor (Module 1
§5). The rhythm history explains *why the patient feels the way they do*; the
HST shows *what the airway is doing.* Present them together and the case makes
sense to the patient.

**3 — Teach circadian hygiene as education, not treatment.** Morning daylight
soon after waking; a consistent wake time seven days a week; dimmer light in the
evening; keeping the last hours before bed unstimulating. These are honest,
low-risk, well-supported timing inputs. Frame them as *supporting the rhythm*,
not as therapy.

**4 — Stay in the structural lane for care.** Airway, posture, cranial and
cervical structure, nasal breathing, and the VAET/CFR-BNS work are what CSA
delivers. The cortisol material is **explanatory**, not a new service line.

**5 — Refer for lab work rather than owning it.** If diurnal cortisol testing is
genuinely warranted, that is a physician's or qualified provider's order and
interpretation — not a chiropractic deliverable, and not a product sale.

## 9. How to say it — scripts

**To a patient (plain language):**

> "Your body runs on a daily clock. Cortisol is one of the signals that clock
> uses — it should be lowest around midnight, start climbing before you wake,
> peak in the first half hour you're up, and taper off all day. When sleep gets
> broken up at night, that pattern flattens out: less of a morning lift, more
> alertness at night. That's a big part of why you feel like you do. My job is
> the structural side — whether your airway has the space to stay open while you
> sleep. That's what the sleep test is going to show us."

**To a DC (peer language):**

> "The AHI tells you *what happened* to the airway. The rhythm history tells you
> *what it cost.* Cortisol timing is a readout of sleep and circadian integrity —
> a flattened diurnal slope, blunted CAR, elevated evening. It's not diagnostic
> for apnea, and the OSA-cortisol literature is genuinely mixed. But it gives you
> the physiology behind the symptom the patient came in with."

**To a dentist or physician (collaborative language):**

> "We're looking at the structural contributors to airway patency, and we track
> the circadian picture because fragmented sleep and misalignment travel
> together. We're not diagnosing and we're not treating the endocrine side —
> that's yours. We just want the structural layer accounted for."

## Key terms (quick glossary)

- **HPA axis** — hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis; the cortisol control loop.
- **SCN** — suprachiasmatic nucleus; the master circadian clock, set by light.
- **CRH / ACTH** — the hypothalamic and pituitary signals upstream of cortisol.
- **CAR** — cortisol awakening response; the sharp rise in the ~30–45 min after
  waking.
- **Diurnal slope** — the rate of decline from morning peak to evening nadir; a
  **flattened** slope is the disrupted pattern.
- **GR / MR** — glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid receptors; different
  affinities, different roles across the curve.
- **Clock genes** — *CLOCK, BMAL1, PER, CRY*; the molecular oscillator cortisol
  helps entrain in peripheral tissue.
- **Circadian misalignment** — central and peripheral clocks, or clock and
  schedule, out of phase.
