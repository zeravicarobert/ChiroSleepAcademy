## Why this workbook exists
Your appliance does its job at the airway. But it doesn't land on an airway — it
lands on a **whole skeleton.** When that skeleton fights the device, you see it as
intolerance, remakes, TMJ complaints, and "it didn't help." Most of those aren't
design flaws. They're **biomechanical mismatches** no one measured.

This primer helps you:
- **reduce device failure**
- **reduce clinician complaints**
- **improve outcomes**
- **differentiate your brand**
- **support interdisciplinary care**

You are not being asked to change what your device does. You're being given the
**missing lens** on the body it acts through.

## Section 1 — Why airway success ≠ biomechanical success
An appliance can open the airway on a bench, in a CBCT, or on night-one titration —
and still fail in the body over weeks. Airway patency is a **local** win. Whether
the patient's **whole structure** accepts and holds that new jaw position is a
**global** question. A device that wins locally but loses globally shows up in your
return and remake numbers. *Success has two scores; most programs only keep one.*

## Section 2 — How mandibular advancement affects cervical load
Advancing the mandible changes the resting pull on the hyoid, the suprahyoid and
infrahyoid muscles, and — through that chain — **cervical posture and load.** A jaw
held forward night after night can shift head-neck mechanics. In a body already
carrying forward-head posture or cervical hypolordosis, that added load is where
morning neck pain, headaches, and "I can't wear it" come from. The device didn't
fail; the **neck it loaded** was never assessed.

## Section 3 — Cranial motion and nasal resistance
Nasal resistance drives mouth breathing, and mouth breathing undermines appliance
outcomes. Cranial and maxillary mechanics influence the nasal airway. When nasal
resistance isn't addressed, a mandibular device is compensating for a problem
upstream of it — a common reason a well-made appliance underperforms. *(Cranial-
motion effects are CSA's model under investigation — presented as a lens to
evaluate, not a settled claim.)*

## Section 4 — Pelvic compensation after appliance use
The body is a connected tension system. A change at the jaw can be **compensated
for far away** — including at the pelvis and sacroiliac joints — because cranial
and pelvic mechanics are linked through the spine and its membranes. When a patient
"just doesn't feel right" on an otherwise good device, a downstream compensation is
often why. It's invisible in the mouth and on a CBCT.

## Section 5 — Posture changes dentists never see
Dentists work in the oral cavity; they rarely photograph standing posture before
and after an appliance. Yet posture is where global adaptation shows up first —
head carriage, shoulder level, pelvic rotation. A simple pre/post postural record
(a Doctor of Chiropractic's routine finding) turns "the patient complained" into
**data your R&D can use.**

## Section 6 — When appliance choice matters biomechanically
Not every patient should get every device. Dental tolerance (occlusion, perio,
restorations, TMJ) is the dentist's call; **biomechanical tolerance** (cervical
load capacity, cranial/pelvic response) is the Doctor of Chiropractic's. The best
device selection is a **co-decision** informed by both. Your product line performs
better when it's matched to the right biomechanical candidate — and worse when it
isn't.

## Section 7 — Feedback manufacturers should request from Doctors of Chiropractic
The Doctor of Chiropractic sees what your clinicians and your telemetry can't: the
**structural response** over weeks. Ask for it. Useful, de-identified feedback:
- pre/post **posture** and cervical-curve notes,
- **TMJ / cervical load** response to advancement,
- signs of **downstream compensation** (pelvic, cranial),
- correlation with **HST** change (AHI/ODI/SpO₂),
- **responder vs. non-responder** patterns by device type.
This is a feedback loop your competitors don't have.

## Section 8 — Designing devices with biomechanical awareness
Carry the lens into design: titration ranges that respect cervical load; guidance
that pairs your device with nasal-airway and postural screening; instructions that
invite co-management rather than assume the mouth is the whole story. Devices
designed with the whole skeleton in mind **fail less and get recommended more.**
