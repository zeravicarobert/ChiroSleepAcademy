# CSA Reference Note — Myofascial Release & the TMJ

**Source gleaned:** "Myofascial Release and TMJ Therapy," by Barry R. Gillespie,
D.M.D., M.S.D., and John F. Barnes, P.T. (undated practitioner article; Barnes
is the founder of the John F. Barnes Myofascial Release Approach; Gillespie ran
a Myofascial Release Treatment Center in Paoli, PA).

**What this note is:** a working distillation for CSA — what's useful, where it
plugs into our model, and exactly what we can and cannot say about it in public.
It is **not** a treatment protocol, a certification, or a claim of evidence.

**Where it plugs in:** Module 1 §3 (the structural layer), the Phase 1 cranial-
postural screen (CLAUDE.md §7), and the DC–DDS collaboration lane.

---

## 1. Read the source honestly first

Before we borrow anything from it, be clear about what it is:

- **It's a practitioner/seminar article, not research.** It ends by announcing a
  nationwide seminar the authors designed together. That doesn't make it wrong —
  it makes it *marketing-adjacent clinical opinion*, and we cite it as opinion.
- **It carries one citation:** Katake (1961), on the tensile and bursting
  strength of human fascia. That is a **cadaveric material-strength measurement**
  — how much load fascia can withstand before failing. The article turns it into
  "fascia can exert a pressure of approximately 2,000 pounds per square inch,"
  which is a different claim entirely. **CSA does not repeat the 2,000 psi
  line.** It's the kind of number that sounds impressive to a lay audience and
  gets a doctor laughed out of a room with an MD in it. (CLAUDE.md §5 — never
  exaggerate evidence.)
- **Costen's syndrome (1930s)** is named as the origin point of the field. Fine
  as history; Costen's original ear-symptom mechanism has been largely
  superseded. Use it as a date, not as a mechanism.
- **Fascia science has moved on since this was written.** Fascial continuity,
  force transmission, and the fascia's sensory/autonomic role are legitimate and
  actively researched. The *mechanism* by which manual pressure changes fascia —
  and how much of the effect is neurological rather than mechanical "taffy
  stretching" — is still debated. Teach the anatomy confidently; hold the
  mechanism loosely.

**Net:** the anatomical premise is worth keeping. The rhetoric isn't.

---

## 2. The one idea worth keeping

> **The TMJ is not a closed system.** It is a node in a continuous soft-tissue
> network that runs head to toe, and strain generated somewhere else in that
> network can land in the jaw.

The article's clinical example is the one every DC already knows: a whiplash
patient with **no direct blow to the jaw** who develops jaw, ear, head, and neck
symptoms anyway. Cervical and thoracic strain travels through connected fascial
sheaths into the cranium and mandible.

This is the same argument CSA already makes about the airway, applied one joint
over. It is *directly* on-model for us:

- **Skull-down** — the article works from the temporo-occiput and maxilla down
  through the mandible to the hyoid. Same direction we teach.
- **Structure is regional, not local** — a jaw problem may be a neck problem, a
  posture problem, or an old-trauma problem.
- **The chiropractor's lane** — this is soft-tissue and structural reasoning, not
  diagnosis of a sleep disorder. It fits inside our scope line.

## 3. The five zones — and how they map to the CSA screen

The article names five areas to assess and treat. Each one has a real airway or
sleep relevance for us. This is the most usable part of the source.

| Their zone | Their point | CSA relevance |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Temporo-occipital** (just posterior to the TMJ) | Chronically involved, especially post-accident; tension here shifts the final bite position | Cranial base + upper cervical — already in our Phase 1 visual screen (occipital). Head/neck position changes airway caliber (Module 1 §3). |
| **2. Mandibular** | Strain here prevents a calm, unstrained jaw position | Mandibular position sets tongue position. A retruded/strained mandible carries the tongue back toward the airway. |
| **3. Maxillary** | The upper half of the TMJ complex must be unstrained too | The maxilla is the airway's ceiling. Narrow/retruded midface = crowded nasal and oropharyngeal space. Ties straight to CBCT findings (Step 3). |
| **4. Hyoid / suprahyoid** | "Often a forgotten area" — suprahyoid tension pulls the mandible in a pathologic direction | **The most airway-relevant zone in the list.** Hyoid position is an airway-collapsibility marker (Module 1 §3), and the suprahyoids are the mechanical link between tongue, hyoid, and jaw. |
| **5. The TMJ complex itself** | The disc is soft-tissue-attached; strain patterns can drive abnormal disc function; try conservative care first | Conservative-before-invasive is our posture too. Also the natural handoff point to an airway-aware DDS. |

**Use it this way:** as a *checklist discipline* for the jaw portion of the
Phase 1 structural airway exam — don't examine "the TMJ," examine the temporo-
occiput, the mandible, the maxilla, the hyoid, and the joint. The hyoid line is
the one most DCs skip and the one most relevant to breathing.

## 4. The technique they describe (summarized, with the caveats)

Extraoral, two-phase, patient supine. Reported faithfully so we know what's
being taught — **not** as a CSA protocol.

**Setup.** Patient supine (or the dental chair reclined fully), knees supported;
if there's forward head posture, support the head with a pillow or two so the
neck isn't held in extension. Operator sits at the head of the table. Any TMJ
appliance stays **in** during treatment.

**Phase I — Compression.** Fingers are hooked into the anti-gonial notches at
the angle of the mandible as a handhold, and gentle cephalad pressure is applied.
*(Note: the copy of the article we have breaks off mid-description here — the
Phase I paragraph gives the handhold and the cautions but never completes the
compression instruction. Anyone reproducing this needs the full original or
hands-on instruction; don't reconstruct it from context.)*

**Phase II — Decompression.** Pressure is released slowly. Hands go flat on the
sides of the face just anterior to the ears over the TMJ, fingertips pointing
toward the feet, with light medial pressure so the hands don't slide on skin.
Gentle caudad pressure is then applied until motion stops at the first "fascial
barrier." The barrier is **not forced** — sustained, gentle pressure is held
until a softening and further motion occur, then held at the next barrier.
**Three to four minutes or longer**, without releasing. Repeated over a series
of visits.

**Always release slowly** to avoid a muscular rebound. Follow with posture and
body-mechanics instruction and an individualized exercise program to hold the
range of motion gained.

**Their stated cautions and contraindications:**
- **Do not perform** if cephalad pressure causes discomfort, or if the patient
  has locking from meniscus dysfunction. Retrodiscal tissue can be acutely
  inflamed and exquisitely tender.
- **Contraindications:** unhealed fracture, osteoporosis, tumor, recent surgical
  repair, active inflammatory process, or a medically unstable patient.
- The authors themselves say the write-up is only a starting point and that the
  feel has to be learned hands-on from an experienced instructor.

**Three CSA notes on top of theirs:**

1. **Scope of practice is state-specific.** The described technique is
   **extraoral**, which keeps it inside what most DCs already do with soft
   tissue. Intraoral TMJ work is a different question and is regulated
   differently state to state. Any DC in CSA verifies their own state board's
   position before doing intraoral work. We never teach around that.
2. **"You will never injure your patient" is overstated.** The article says
   following the contraindications guarantees safety. No technique carries a
   guarantee. Screen, document, and stop when the tissue or the patient says
   stop.
3. **Learn it properly or don't do it.** A paragraph is not training. If Robert
   wants this in the CSA toolkit, it comes from a Barnes-lineage MFR course or
   an equivalent hands-on program, and CSA says so plainly.

## 5. What this does for the CSA model

**a) It reinforces the structural argument with a second mechanism.**
We already teach bone, posture, and position. Fascial continuity adds *tension
transmission* — a way to explain why a patient's jaw, airway, and neck problems
travel together and why a purely local fix underperforms. That's a peer-level
explanation, not a hype line.

**b) It strengthens the hyoid story.**
The suprahyoid zone is the cleanest bridge in the whole article: it's a fascial
finding, a jaw finding, a tongue finding, and an airway finding at the same time.
Worth a dedicated segment in Module 1 §3.

**c) It's a credible bridge to the dental sleep world.**
A DMD and a PT co-authored this. The technique explicitly keeps the patient's
**oral appliance in place** — the soft-tissue work supports the appliance rather
than competing with it. That is exactly the collaborative posture CSA needs for
Sal Rodas, the Foundation for Airway Health, and the airway-aware DDS bench
(CLAUDE.md §15). It's a good talking point precisely because it's *not* a
territory grab.

**d) It's a possible language layer for VAET.**
If fascial strain vocabulary fits how Dr. Z already explains what VAET/CFR-BNS is
doing, this gives a shared vocabulary with PTs, DOs, and dentists.
**[DR. Z — decide]** Does CSA adopt fascial language formally, or keep it as
background reasoning? Adopting it means owning the mechanism debate in §1.

**e) It sharpens the trauma-history question.**
"Any car accidents, falls, or head injuries — even years ago?" belongs in the
Phase 1 sleep-and-airway history, because the article's central clinical case is
delayed, remote-onset jaw and airway strain from a non-jaw trauma.

## 6. Claims discipline — say / don't say

**Safe to say:**
- "The jaw doesn't exist in isolation. It's connected through continuous
  soft tissue to the neck, the cranial base, and the hyoid — so strain from an
  old whiplash can show up as jaw and airway symptoms years later."
- "When we assess the jaw, we assess the temporo-occiput, the maxilla, the
  mandible, the hyoid, and the joint itself — not just the joint."
- "Soft-tissue and structural work may improve jaw mechanics, range of motion,
  and comfort, and it can support the work an airway-aware dentist is doing."

**Do not say:**
- Any version of "fascia exerts 2,000 psi." (Misreads a tensile-strength study.)
- That myofascial release treats, reduces, or cures sleep apnea, or lowers AHI.
  There is no basis here for that, and it crosses a red line (CLAUDE.md §5).
- That releasing fascia "fixes the bite." The article claims fascial tension
  influences final occlusal position; **occlusion is the dentist's call**, and
  we don't pronounce on it.
- Anything implying MFR replaces an oral appliance, CPAP, or a physician's
  evaluation.
