# CSA Clinical Brief — ADHD, Anger, and the Airway

**Audience:** Chiropractors training through Chiropractic Sleep Academy (CSA).
**Where it sits:** A **communication and screening brief**, not a treatment
protocol. Module 1 gives you the anatomy; this gives you the conversation —
specifically the one that starts when a patient says *"I have ADHD"* or *"I have
a temper problem"* and nobody has ever looked at how they breathe at night.

> **The scope line, stated once and meant throughout this brief:**
> Chiropractors **do not diagnose** ADHD. Chiropractors **do not diagnose**
> obstructive sleep apnea. Chiropractors **do not treat** ADHD, and nothing in
> this brief is a reason for any patient to stop, reduce, or question a
> medication or a psychiatric diagnosis. Your role is **structural**: screen,
> educate, refer, and deliver structural care. This brief exists to make you
> *fluent*, not to make you a diagnostician.

---

## 1. Why this brief exists

Content like this circulates constantly in ADHD communities online:

> *"Sensory overload makes you rage. You lose it worst with the people you love.
> The anger passes fast but the shame lasts days. You barely remember what you
> said. You apologize constantly and it keeps happening. That's not a character
> flaw — it's a nervous system that responds to rejection like it's real
> danger."*

Your patients are reading this. Some of them are reading it at 1 a.m. because
they can't sleep. It resonates because it describes something real: they *are*
irritable, they *are* volatile, and they *do* feel ashamed afterward.

Here is what almost none of that content asks: **how is this person breathing
for the eight hours before the morning they lose their temper?**

That question is CSA's contribution. Not a competing explanation — an
**unexamined variable**.

---

## 2. What the post gets right, and where to be careful

You need to be able to hold both sides of this honestly. If a patient quotes
that post to you, you don't dismiss it and you don't inflate it.

**Well supported:**
- ADHD involves genuine difficulty with emotional regulation, not just
  attention. Emotional dysregulation is a well-described feature of the
  condition.
- Sensory load consumes regulatory capacity. Reducing input lowers reactivity.
- Sleep problems and ADHD travel together at high rates, in both directions.

**Described but less formally established:**
- **"Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria" (RSD)** is a clinical description
  popularized by Dr. William Dodson. It is *not* a DSM-5 diagnosis and does not
  yet have the research base that ADHD itself has. It's a useful word for a real
  experience — treat it as a description, not a diagnosis.
- The claim that memory "shuts down" during an outburst is a loose retelling of
  something real (high arousal degrades encoding), stated more confidently than
  the evidence supports.

**Where the post is simply silent:**
- It never asks about sleep. Not once. And several of the exact features it
  lists — low frustration tolerance, morning irritability, emotional volatility,
  foggy recall — are also the everyday signature of **fragmented, non-restorative
  sleep.**

That silence is the opening. Not to contradict the post. To complete it.

## 3. The mechanism: what disturbed sleep does to emotional control

This is the physiology to have in your head. Explain it plainly; never overstate
it.

**Sleep loss raises the threat response and lowers the brake.**
Sleep-deprivation research using functional imaging has shown amygdala
reactivity to negative stimuli increases while its functional connection to
prefrontal regulatory areas weakens. Practical translation: the alarm gets
louder and the part that talks you down gets quieter. That is, mechanistically,
a very good description of "I went from zero to yelling."

**Fragmented sleep is not the same as short sleep — and may matter more.**
A person with sleep-disordered breathing may be in bed nine hours and still be
pulled out of deep sleep dozens of times an hour by arousals they never
consciously register. They report *"I sleep fine, I'm just tired and cranky."*
They are not lying. They genuinely don't know.

**Every apneic or hypopneic event is a sympathetic event.**
Oxygen desaturation and arousal drive a stress response — repeatedly, all night.
Someone who has run that pattern for years starts the day with less regulatory
reserve than they'd have otherwise. There is nothing mystical about why they're
short-tempered by 4 p.m.

**Sleep is when emotional experience gets processed and consolidated.**
Sleep — REM in particular — is heavily involved in memory consolidation and in
processing emotional material. Chronically disrupt it and yesterday's
frustrations arrive at today less digested.

**Note the overlap deliberately.** Irritability, poor frustration tolerance,
inattention, forgetfulness, emotional volatility, low mood: that list is a
recognized presentation of untreated sleep-disordered breathing, and it overlaps
substantially with what the ADHD post describes. **Overlap is not identity.**
Both can be true in the same patient. Usually, in the patients you'll see, both
*are* true.

## 4. What the sleep-and-airway literature actually supports

Be precise here — this is where credibility is won or lost.

**In children:** Snoring and sleep-disordered breathing are associated with
inattention, hyperactivity, and behavioral problems. This association is
consistent across multiple studies (Chervin and colleagues did much of the
foundational work). It is a well-established *association*.

**And the honest limit:** The CHAT trial (NEJM, 2013) randomized children with
sleep apnea to early adenotonsillectomy versus watchful waiting. Surgery clearly
improved sleep study findings, symptoms, quality of life, and caregiver- and
teacher-rated behavior — but it did **not** show a significant advantage on the
study's primary neurocognitive measure of attention and executive function.

Say that out loud when you teach this. It is the difference between a clinician
and a marketer. *Treating the airway improved how these kids slept, felt, and
behaved — it did not simply erase the attention findings.* That is a real,
meaningful, honest result, and it's more persuasive than an overstated one.

**In adults:** Obstructive sleep apnea is associated with daytime sleepiness,
impaired executive function, irritability, and depressive symptoms. Treatment of
OSA is associated with improvement in daytime function and mood in many
patients. Again — association and improvement, not cure of a separate
psychiatric condition.

**On comorbidity:** People with ADHD have elevated rates of sleep disorders,
including sleep-disordered breathing, restless legs, and delayed sleep phase.
The relationship runs both ways. Which is exactly why *"you have ADHD"* should
never be the end of the sleep conversation — it should be a reason to have it.

> citation against the primary source.** The summaries above are accurate as
> teaching-level statements; a public claim needs the paper in hand.

## 5. Red lines for this topic specifically

Section 5 of CSA's standards applies in full. These are the additional ones this
subject demands:

**Never say or imply:**
- That ADHD is "really just" sleep apnea, or that the diagnosis is fake,
  overdiagnosed, or a failure of medicine.
- That chiropractic, VAET, or airway care treats, manages, or improves ADHD.
- That a patient should stop, reduce, delay, or reconsider a medication. That
  conversation belongs to the prescribing physician, and it is not a
  conversation you are invited to.
- That you can tell from an exam whether someone has sleep apnea. You cannot.
  A physician reads the test.
- That fixing the airway will fix a marriage, a temper, or a life.

**Extra care with this population.** Someone in the shame cycle that post
describes is vulnerable. They are primed to hear "there's one real cause and
here it is." Do not be the person who gives them a new totalizing story. Give
them a variable nobody checked, and a way to check it.

**Never turn a parent against their child's care team.** With pediatric cases,
the entire posture is: *let's make sure the sleep piece has been looked at, and
let's loop in your pediatrician.* Never *"the doctors missed this."*

## 6. The clinical move

When emotional dysregulation, irritability, or an ADHD history shows up in your
intake, add sleep and airway to the picture. Screening is in your scope.

**Ask the adult patient:**
- Do you snore? Has anyone told you that you stop breathing, gasp, or choke?
- Do you wake unrefreshed no matter how long you're in bed?
- Do you wake with a dry mouth, sore throat, or headache?
- Do you grind or clench your teeth?
- Do you wake to urinate more than once a night?
- Is your irritability worse on the mornings after your worst nights?
- Have you ever had a sleep study? CPAP? An oral appliance?

**Ask the parent about a child:**
- Does your child snore, breathe through their mouth, or sleep restlessly?
- Mouth open during the day? Chapped lips? Dark under-eye circles?
- Tonsils or adenoids ever flagged?
- Bedwetting past the expected age?
- Are the behavior reports worse on days after bad nights?

**Use the instruments already in the CSA pathway:** Epworth, STOP-BANG, the CSA
questionnaire, SnoreLab or equivalent app tracking, Mallampati, tongue
scalloping, and the visual cranial-postural screen (frontal, zygomatic,
TMJ/earlobe, occipital, forward head posture).

**Then follow the pathway, not your hunch:**

> Consult → Structural Airway Exam → **Home Sleep Test** → Report of Findings

The HST is ordered through the CSA partner pathway. **A physician reads it.**
Your job is to coordinate the order, review the report through the structural
lens, and integrate it. Refer out where it belongs — DDS for appliance
candidacy, ENT for tonsils/adenoids or nasal obstruction, sleep physician for
diagnosis and medical management, pediatrician for a child. Deliver structural
care in your lane.

**If the test is negative, you say so, and you say it clearly.** A patient with
a normal HST and a genuine ADHD diagnosis now has one fewer unknown and a
clinician who told them the truth. That is a good outcome and you should present
it as one.

## 7. Language that works

**Say this:**
> "Everything you're describing is real, and I'm not going to talk you out of
> your diagnosis. I want to check one thing nobody seems to have checked — how
> you're actually breathing while you sleep. Poor sleep and a compromised airway
> can produce a lot of what you just described: short fuse, foggy memory, no
> patience by the afternoon. It doesn't replace what you already know about
> yourself. It might be sitting on top of it and making all of it harder. Let's
> find out instead of guessing."

**And this, on the structural piece:**
> "My part of this is structural — how your head, jaw, neck, and posture affect
> the space you breathe through. I'm not treating ADHD and I'm not diagnosing
> sleep apnea. A physician reads the test. What I can do is make sure the
> structural side has actually been looked at, and work on it where it's
> contributing."

**Never say this:**
> ~~"Your ADHD is probably really sleep apnea."~~
> ~~"I've seen kids get off their meds after we opened up their airway."~~
> ~~"If we fix your airway, the anger goes away."~~

The first two are false. The third is a promise you cannot keep, and the person
you'd be making it to has already been let down enough.

## 8. Where this fits in CSA

This brief is a **front-door conversation**, not a new offer. Emotional
dysregulation, irritability, and "I've always had ADHD" are among the most
common ways an untested airway walks into a chiropractic office wearing a
different label. Recognizing it is a screening skill.

It routes into the existing pathway with nothing new bolted on:

- **Step 1 — Alignment Call.** For a DC who sees themselves in this.
- **Step 2 — Sleep Baseline ($300).** The HST. The objective data.
- **Step 3 — Airway Baseline ($500).** CBCT and airway-focused review.
- **Step 4+ —** Only where the findings and the case actually warrant it.

The one-line version, for a DC and for a patient alike:

> **Before you accept that this is only how your brain is built — let's make
> sure we know how you breathe at night.**
