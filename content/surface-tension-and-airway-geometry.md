# Surface Tension, Airway Geometry, and the Structural Question

**Audience:** Clinical. Written to be read by a chiropractor, a dentist, an ENT,
or a sleep physician without any of them having to discount it.
**Status:** Hypothesis document. States a mechanism, cites the literature behind
it, names the part that is not yet tested, and names what would disprove it.

> **Scope line, stated once and meant throughout:**
> Chiropractors do not diagnose obstructive sleep apnea. Nothing here claims
> that structural care treats or cures OSA. This document argues a narrower
> point: that airway geometry is a plausible and untested modifier of a fluid
> force that the sleep literature has already shown to be real.

---

## 1. The core principle

Obstructive sleep apnea is multifactorial. Pressure, muscle tone, tissue
compliance, arousal threshold, loop gain, fluid behavior, and sleep-state
physiology all contribute.

All of them operate inside anatomy.

**Physiology lives inside anatomy.**

Anatomy is not the only cause of airway collapse. It is the physical container
within which every other factor has to act. That distinction is the entire
argument, and it is deliberately modest: it concedes everything sleep medicine
says about multifactorial causation and still leaves a structural question
standing.

The proposed chain:

**Structure → Geometry → Wet-tissue proximity → Fluid/interface forces → Airway mechanics**

---

## 2. Surface tension in the airway is established, not speculative

This is the part most structural clinicians do not know, and it is the strongest
ground CSA has.

**Jason Kirkness and colleagues measured it.** Their work established that the
liquid layer lining the upper airway generates surface tension, and that this
surface tension influences whether the airway stays open.

Two anchor papers:

- Kirkness JP, et al. *Relationship between surface tension of upper airway
  lining liquid and upper airway collapsibility during sleep in obstructive
  sleep apnea hypopnea syndrome.* **J Appl Physiol**, 2003. (PMID 12857768)
- Kirkness JP, et al. *Surface tension of upper airway mucosal lining liquid in
  obstructive sleep apnea/hypopnea syndrome.* **Sleep**, 2005;28(4):457–463.
  (PMID 16171290)

What that literature describes:

- The mucosal lining liquid **between the soft palate and the tongue** produces
  an **adhesion force** that resists the soft palate separating from the tongue.
- **Lowering** the surface tension of upper airway lining liquid **reduces upper
  airway opening and closing pressures.**
- Topical application of exogenous surfactant to the pharyngeal mucosa lowers
  surface tension, **reduces upper airway collapsibility during sleep,** and
  produces a **reduction in the severity of sleep-disordered breathing.**

Read that last point carefully. It is the load-bearing fact in this document.
The force is not hypothetical. It was measured, it was manipulated, and
manipulating it changed a clinical outcome.

**Honest note on where the field stands.** This is not settled science with a
single agreed magnitude. A 2003 *Journal of Applied Physiology* piece is titled
*"Upper airway surface tension: is it a significant cause of airflow obstruction
during sleep?"* — the question mark is the field's own. Surface tension is one
contributing force among several, and how much it matters in any given patient
is still argued. Present it that way. Overstating it is the fastest way to lose
the room.

---

## 3. The elementary picture

Two wet microscope slides.

Put a thin layer of water between them and they become surprisingly hard to pull
apart. The water creates an adhesive effect. Water molecules attract one another,
and the closer two wet surfaces sit, the more those fluid forces have the
opportunity to matter.

The upper airway is not a dry pipe. It is a wet, living, collapsible structure
lined with fluid. When opposing moist tissue surfaces come very close together,
the thin fluid layers can connect and form a liquid bridge — the same principle
as the slides.

Kirkness's group named the specific interface: **soft palate against tongue.**

---

## 4. Why geometry is the untested variable

Here is the pivot, and it needs to be stated precisely.

The same saliva, with the same chemistry and the same measured surface tension,
can produce a different mechanical outcome depending on airway geometry. If two
wet tissues are far apart, surface tension has little opportunity to act on them.
If those same tissues are nearly touching, it may become mechanically important.

**Same fluid. Different structure. Different geometry. Different mechanical outcome.**

Now the critical distinction, and the one line in this document most likely to be
misquoted:

> Changing structure does **not** change the chemistry of the saliva and does
> **not** lower surface tension itself.
>
> Changing geometry may reduce the **mechanical consequences** of surface tension
> by increasing separation between wet tissues.

Never compress that into "realignment reduces surface tension." It is false, it
is checkable, and a sleep physician will use it to end the conversation.

The correct framing:

**Structural alignment determines, in part, how close wet tissues get — and
therefore how much opportunity surface tension has to matter.**

### What nobody has run

The surfactant researchers changed **the fluid** and saw airway collapsibility
drop. Nobody has tested whether changing **the geometry** weakens the same
adhesion force by increasing the distance across which it has to act.

That is the open question. It is specific, it is testable, and it belongs to
structural clinicians because nobody else is positioned to ask it.

## 5. Negative pressure and the contact threshold

On inhalation, pressure inside parts of the upper airway becomes more negative,
pulling inward on a collapsible airway.

- If the airway is already narrow or crowded, **less** additional inward force is
  required before opposing tissues contact one another.
- If the airway begins with more space, **more** inward force is required before
  those tissues meet.
- Once wet tissues meet, surface tension becomes one additional force resisting
  their separation.

**Structure → proximity → pressure susceptibility → tissue contact → surface-tension effect**

This is why geometry is not a cosmetic variable. It sets the threshold at which
the fluid force gets a chance to participate at all.

---

## 6. A more favorable structural position

There is likely no single measurable "perfect" cranial position for every person.
A more defensible concept is a **more favorable anatomical or functional
position.**

When structure is compressed, rotated, displaced, or poorly organized, opposing
wet tissues may be brought closer together. When structural relationships
improve, anatomy may be able to express a more favorable geometry.

**Better structural relationship → greater tissue separation → less opportunity
for wet tissues to approximate → less opportunity for surface tension to matter**

**Alignment gives anatomy room to express better geometry.**

## 7. The other fluid story: rostral fluid shift

This mechanism deserves a place in any structural airway argument, and it is
better documented than any speculative water physics.

During the day, fluid pools in the lower extremities under gravity. On lying
down at night, it redistributes rostrally to the neck, where it narrows the upper
airway and increases collapsibility.

**Stefania Redolfi and colleagues** found that in non-obese men, the overnight
decrease in leg fluid volume correlated strongly with the overnight increase in
neck circumference — and with the AHI, explaining roughly **64% of AHI
variability, independent of BMI.**

Why this matters to CSA:

1. It is a **fluid** mechanism, so it sits naturally alongside the surface
   tension argument.
2. It is **positional and postural**, which is much closer to the structural
   lane than any molecular-water theory.
3. It is **well published**, so citing it costs nothing in credibility and buys
   a great deal.

A structural clinician who can discuss rostral fluid shift fluently is speaking
the sleep world's own language.

## 8. What would falsify this

A hypothesis that names its own test reads as science. One that does not reads
as belief. This is the section that makes the difference.

**The claim under test:** improving structural relationships increases separation
between opposing wet airway tissues, reducing the mechanical contribution of
surface tension and lowering the likelihood or duration of obstructive events.

**What CSA can actually measure, using the existing pathway:**

| Measure | Instrument | When |
|---|---|---|
| AHI, ODI, oxygen nadir, event duration | Home sleep test | Baseline and follow-up |
| Airway volume, minimum cross-sectional area | CBCT | Baseline and follow-up |
| Subjective sleep quality, daytime sleepiness | Epworth, CSA questionnaire | Baseline and follow-up |
| Snoring intensity/duration | SnoreLab or equivalent | Continuous |

**The hypothesis is weakened or falsified if:**

- Structural intervention produces **no measurable change in airway geometry**
  on CBCT. If geometry does not move, the mechanism has no starting point.
- Airway geometry **does** improve on imaging but **AHI/ODI do not change.** This
  would suggest geometry is not the limiting variable in these patients.
- Improvements appear but do not exceed what would be expected from weight
  change, positional change, nasal patency change, or measurement variability.
- Results appear only in unblinded subjective reports and vanish in the objective
  HST data.

**Confounders that must be tracked, not ignored:** body weight change, sleeping
position, alcohol, nasal congestion, medication changes, night-to-night AHI
variability (which is substantial), and placebo/expectancy effects in a
high-touch care model.

Stating these up front is not weakness. It is the thing that separates a clinical
research question from a marketing claim, and it is what makes a
research-literate dentist or physician willing to keep listening.

## 9. What a chiropractor does with this

The mechanism is interesting. The clinical hook is what makes it useful.

**Who this applies to.** Patients with structural crowding of the airway —
narrow maxilla, retruded mandible, high/narrow palatal vault, forward head
posture, cranial asymmetry, chronic mouth-breathing, tongue scalloping, elevated
Mallampati score — who also report sleep and airway symptoms.

**What to look for.** The Module 1 structural screen: frontal, zygomatic,
TMJ/earlobe, occipital, forward head posture. Plus the airway-specific findings:
Mallampati, tongue scalloping, nasal patency, palatal vault shape.

**What to do.** Screen, educate, refer for the home sleep test, get the physician
read, and place structural care in its correct position — as a potential modifier
of the container, not as a treatment for the diagnosis.

**What to say to the patient.** "Your airway is a wet, collapsible space, and the
structure around it affects how much room it has. We are not treating your sleep
apnea. We are addressing the structural framework it operates inside, and we are
going to measure whether that makes a difference."

**What to never say.** That structural care treats, manages, or cures obstructive
sleep apnea. That realignment lowers surface tension. That the HST is a
chiropractic test. That any of this replaces CPAP, an oral appliance, or a
physician's care.

## 10. The VAET application

VAET is the structural intervention CSA proposes as the geometry variable.

The working concept is **not** that VAET changes saliva chemistry or directly
lowers surface tension. The concept is that structural intervention may change
geometry; that changing geometry may change how closely wet airway tissues
approximate; and that changing wet-tissue proximity may change how much
opportunity surface tension and other fluid-interface forces have to influence
airway mechanics.

**Structure → Geometry → Proximity → Fluid/interface forces → Airway mechanics**

This does not mean anatomy is the only factor in obstructive sleep apnea. It
means anatomy is the physical framework within which the other factors operate.

**Physiology lives inside anatomy.**

---

## 11. The question, stated for a research audience

> Established work shows that surface tension in the upper airway lining liquid
> contributes to adhesion between the soft palate and tongue, and that reducing
> that surface tension pharmacologically reduces airway collapsibility and the
> severity of sleep-disordered breathing.
>
> That work altered the fluid. It did not alter the geometry.
>
> **If structural intervention can increase the separation between opposing wet
> airway surfaces, does it reduce the mechanical contribution of that same
> adhesion force — and does that change measurable outcomes on home sleep
> testing?**

That is a specific, testable, unanswered question. It is worth investigating, and
CSA is positioned to investigate it.

## Appendix A — Teaching illustrations (label as illustration, not evidence)

**The two wet slides.** The clearest physical demonstration of the adhesion
principle. Use it every time.

**The rainbow in the high-speed water-droplet video.** When a droplet strikes
water, surface tension shapes the water into a very thin, curved film. Light
reflects off both the front and back surfaces of that film. Because the film's
thickness varies, some wavelengths reinforce and others cancel — **thin-film
interference**, the same effect as a soap bubble or an oil sheen.

The rainbow is not caused by "surface tensions colliding."

**Surface tension shapes the water. Light interacts with that shape. Color
reveals the geometry.**

It is a beautiful demonstration that surface tension controls *form*. It is an
analogy about shape, not evidence about airways. Say so when you use it.

## Appendix B — A simple at-home practice

The goal is not to have anyone experiment with surfactants, oils, or soaps in
their airway. A simpler approach uses normal function: palate, oral cavity,
voice, vibration, airflow, and pressure change.

**Sing. Hum.**

Singing and humming produce vibration through the oral and craniofacial spaces,
repeated movement of the tongue and soft palate, changing pressures in the oral
and upper-airway spaces, airflow, and resonance.

Humming also has real supporting evidence for one specific effect: **Weitzberg
and Lundberg (2002)** showed nasal nitric oxide increased roughly **15-fold**
during humming versus quiet exhalation, through enhanced sinus ventilation.

**State the limits plainly:** these practices are not established treatments for
obstructive sleep apnea and are not proven to reposition cranial bones. The nasal
NO finding is real, but its relevance to OSA outcomes is indirect and unproven.
They are simple, safe ways to use voice, breath, vibration, pressure, and palatal
function together.
