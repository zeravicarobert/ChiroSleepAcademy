# CSA Consultation Script

*Structural Airway Chiropractic — the front door of the clinical
relationship. CSA adaptation of the AMC Consultation (Tom Owen III).*

> **Voice & guardrails.** Consult = **Ear**. You listen; you speak half as
> much as you want to. No cure claims, no fear-selling, no diagnosing OSA.
> Be exclamatory only about findings that are real. Protect the patient's
> interest over the sale.

---

## Teaching notes (for the doctor, before the room)

The consultation is the cornerstone. Everything downstream — the exam,
the HST, the Report of Findings — depends on whether the person in front
of you feels **heard** and **understood** in the first seven minutes. In
sleep and airway work this matters even more than in a pain practice,
because the patient has usually been tired for years and told "it's
normal" or "just lose weight." You are often the first person to take
their sleep seriously.

**The five things a CSA consultation accomplishes:**

1. **They like you and trust you.** People don't stay for results alone;
   they stay because they feel respected and understood.
2. **They know you understand them.** The #1 complaint in healthcare is
   "my doctor doesn't listen." Prove you do.
3. **You get a true history** — sleep, airway, and structure.
4. **They agree to the next step** — a Structural Airway Exam and,
   objectively, a Home Sleep Test — because it's the honest way to find
   out what's going on. (You don't need to sell it; you frame it.)
5. **You do it in about 5–7 minutes.** Efficient, unhurried, present.

**The three crucial parts:** Intro · Identify the problem · Close.

## The script

### Intro (servant handshake, unhurried, eye contact)

> "Ms. \[Name]? I'm Dr. Zeravica. It's a real pleasure to meet you."
> *(Set your things down. Sit. File down, pen down, hands clasped, lean
> in, turn your ear toward her.)*
> "Ms. \[Name] — how can I help you today?"

*(Now you go quiet. She talks. You listen like an attorney taking notes
you'll prove you heard, days from now, at the Report.)*

### History — sleep, airway, structure

Let her open the door, then walk through it gently, conversationally
(filler words, not an interrogation):

> "When you say you're exhausted — how long has that been going on?
> …Months? Years?"
>
> "Tell me about your nights. Do you snore? Has anyone ever told you that
> you stop breathing, or gasp, or choke in your sleep?"
>
> "Do you wake up with a dry mouth, or a headache in the morning?"
>
> "How's your daytime? Do you fight to stay awake — driving, reading, at
> your desk after lunch?" *(This is the Epworth conversation, in plain
> language.)*
>
> "Do you breathe through your nose, or your mouth — day and night?"
>
> "Who sleeps next to you? What do *they* say about your sleep?"
> *(The bed partner is your best witness. Note this — you'll want them at
> the Report.)*

**The present-and-interested moment** (three or four questions in — not
first, not last): push the file away, and ask the thing she most wants a
doctor to ask:

> "Before we go further — would you just tell me what a tired day *costs*
> you? What are you not able to do, that you'd like to do?"

*(This is the honest version of AMC's "need behind the need." You are not
mining it to close her — you are learning what her sleep is stealing, so
you can serve her. Let her answer land. Then:)*

> "So it isn't really about the snoring, is it? It's about \[the mornings
> with your kids / the drive home you're scared of / the person you used
> to be]. That's why you and I aren't going to take this lightly."

### Prior care & honest cost comparison

> "Have you been checked for this before — a sleep study, a CPAP, an
> appliance, anything?"
>
> "Did it help? Are you still tired?" *(down-tone, unhurried)*
>
> "What's this cost you so far — in money, and in years of feeling this
> way?"

*(Cost comparison stays — but honest. The point is never "you overspent,
buy from me." The point is: you deserve to actually find the cause, not
keep paying for tired.)*

### Screen for progression — honestly, without fear-selling

> "Are you noticing anything else stacking up — high blood pressure,
> weight that won't move, mood, memory, heartburn at night?"

*(These are *associations* worth noting, not verdicts. If she asks
whether her sleep is "dangerous," tell the truth: untreated
sleep-disordered breathing is *linked* to real health risks, which is
exactly why we don't guess — we test. Never predict a heart attack.)*

### The close (frame the next step — no pressure)

> "Ms. \[Name], here's how we work. First, I need to find out whether this
> is even something structural airway care can help — some of it is, some
> of it needs another table, and I'll be honest with you either way.
>
> Second, I don't yet know what's actually happening while you sleep, and
> it's clear no one's shown you. So I don't want to guess — guessing about
> your health is the one thing we don't do here.
>
> So what I'd like to do is look at your airway and your structure
> carefully, and then get objective data on your actual sleep — the way an
> X-ray gives objective data on a spine. Once I have that, I can tell you
> honestly what I *can* help with, what I can't, and where else you might
> want to go. Does that sound fair?"

*(She says yes — because it's honest and it's for her. She won't ask "how
much is the sleep test" mid-consult, because you've made this about
finding the truth, not buying a product. Price is handled cleanly, at the
right moment, by your team.)*

> "Follow me."

## Debrief — what just happened (highlights)

- **Servant handshake, unhurried intro, eye contact.** For every action,
  an equal reaction: warmth earns warmth.
- **File down, ear turned, you listened.** You were the Ear.
- **The interested moment** (what does tired cost you) was placed in the
  middle — the human center of the whole visit. Used to *serve*, not to
  close.
- **The bed partner** was identified — your clinical witness and your
  Report-day ally.
- **Cost comparison and progression** were handled *honestly* — no fear,
  no verdicts, routed toward objective testing.
- **The close framed the HST as the honest next step**, not a product.
  The DC does not diagnose; the objective data will, with the reading
  physician. You are the guide, not the guesser.

**Never:** promise a cure, predict catastrophe to motivate a yes, imply
you diagnose sleep apnea, or let the sale outrank the patient. If any of
those creep in, you've left CSA and gone back to selling.
