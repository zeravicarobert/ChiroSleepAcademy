# CSA Academy — Module 1: Anatomy & Physiology

**Audience:** Chiropractors training through Chiropractic Sleep Academy (CSA).
**Where it sits:** The **foundation.** Before you can communicate a case
(Module 2) or present a plan (Module 3), you have to understand the airway, the
structure around it, and the physiology of sleep. This module gives you the
language and the mental model — so you speak like a credible clinician and never
drift out of the structural lane.

> **The scope line, stated once and meant throughout this module:**
> Chiropractors **do not diagnose** obstructive sleep apnea. A physician reads
> and diagnoses the home sleep test. Your role is **structural**: assess, screen,
> educate, refer, and deliver structural care. Everything below is to make you
> *fluent*, not to make you a diagnostician.

---

## 1. The frame: skull-down, sleep-first

Most sleep-and-airway conversations start at the lungs and work up. We start at
the **skull and the airway** and work **down**. Why: the upper airway is where
most sleep-disordered breathing actually happens, and the *structure* around
that airway — cranium, maxilla, mandible, hyoid, cervical spine, posture — is
exactly the layer a chiropractor is trained to see and influence.

That structural layer is CSA's contribution. We're not competing with CPAP, oral
appliances, or sleep medicine — we're adding the piece the airway conversation
has been missing.

---

## 2. Upper airway anatomy — the collapsible tube

The pharyngeal airway is the one part of the breathing passage with **no rigid
skeletal support.** It stays open by muscle tone and by the structures around
it. When tone drops (in sleep) or the surrounding structure crowds it, it
narrows or collapses. Learn these landmarks cold:

**The nasal route (breathe here, not the mouth):**
- **Nasal passages, turbinates, septum** — a deviated septum or enlarged
  turbinates raise nasal resistance and push people toward mouth-breathing.
- **Nasal breathing** filters, humidifies, and drives proper tongue posture and
  craniofacial development. Chronic mouth-breathing is a structural red flag.

**The pharynx (top to bottom):**
- **Nasopharynx** — behind the nose; adenoids live here (big driver in kids).
- **Oropharynx** — behind the mouth; the **soft palate, uvula, tonsils,** and
  **tongue base** all crowd this space. This is the most common collapse zone.
- **Hypopharynx** — above the larynx; tongue base and epiglottis matter here.

**The key soft-tissue players:**
- **Tongue** — the big one. The **genioglossus** is the main airway-dilating
  muscle; a large tongue (macroglossia) or a tongue that falls back in sleep
  obstructs the airway.
- **Soft palate & uvula** — long/floppy tissue vibrates (snoring) and collapses.
- **Tonsils / adenoids** — enlargement narrows the space, especially in younger
  patients.

**The bony/structural frame (your wheelhouse — Section 3):**
- **Maxilla & mandible** — a narrow maxilla or a retruded mandible
  (**retrognathia**) sets the tongue back and shrinks the airway.
- **Hyoid bone** — a low-positioned hyoid is associated with a more collapsible
  airway.
- **Cranial base & cervical spine** — head and neck position changes airway
  caliber directly.

## 3. The structural layer — where the chiropractor lives

This is the part medicine often under-weights and where you add value. Teach it
as *contribution to airway patency and breathing mechanics* — never as a cure.

- **Craniofacial development.** Form follows function. Chronic mouth-breathing
  and low tongue posture in development are associated with a narrower maxilla,
  high-arched palate, and a smaller airway. This is the developmental story
  behind many adult airways.
- **Maxillary/mandibular position.** A narrow or retruded midface/mandible
  crowds the tongue backward into the airway.
- **Forward head posture (FHP).** Head-forward posture reduces upper-airway
  cross-sectional area and increases collapsibility. Posture is airway.
- **Cervical spine & cranial base.** Position and motion here change airway
  caliber and the neuromuscular tone that holds it open.
- **TMJ & tongue posture.** Jaw position and resting tongue posture tie the
  whole system together.

## 4. The physiology of sleep & breathing

**Normal sleep architecture.** Sleep cycles roughly every ~90 minutes through:
- **N1** — light transition.
- **N2** — the bulk of sleep.
- **N3** — deep, slow-wave, restorative sleep.
- **REM** — dreaming sleep, with **skeletal-muscle atonia.**

**Why sleep matters to the airway:** muscle tone — including the genioglossus
that holds the airway open — **drops during sleep, and drops most in REM.** An
airway that's fine awake can narrow or collapse once that tone falls. That's why
the problem shows up at night and why an awake exam isn't enough — you need the
sleep data.

**The sleep-disordered-breathing spectrum (mild → severe):**
1. **Snoring** — tissue vibration; a structural signal, not benign noise.
2. **Upper Airway Resistance Syndrome (UARS)** — increased effort, fragmented
   sleep, often without full apneas.
3. **Hypopnea** — partial airflow reduction with a drop in oxygen or an arousal.
4. **Apnea** — airflow essentially stops. **Obstructive** (airway collapses
   despite effort — the structural kind) vs **central** (the brain doesn't send
   the signal — a medical kind that refers out).

## 5. The numbers — read a report without bluffing

You won't diagnose from these, but you must understand them to have the
conversation and to explain a physician's report.

- **AHI (Apnea-Hypopnea Index)** — apneas + hypopneas **per hour** of sleep. The
  headline number.
  - **< 5** normal · **5–15** mild · **15–30** moderate · **> 30** severe.
- **RDI (Respiratory Disturbance Index)** — AHI plus effort-related arousals
  (catches UARS the AHI can miss).
- **ODI (Oxygen Desaturation Index)** — how many times per hour SpO₂ drops a set
  amount (commonly ≥3–4%). Your oxygen-dip number.
- **SpO₂ nadir** — the lowest oxygen level of the night.
- **Sleep efficiency / arousal index** — how fragmented the night was.

**How to use them honestly:** anchor the conversation on the patient's *own*
objective data ("your test showed an AHI of ___ and oxygen dipping to ___"),
connect it to the structure you found, and route diagnosis to the reading
physician.

## 6. The screening instruments — how to actually score them

These are **screening and risk-stratification** tools — they flag risk and build
the case for testing. They are **not** a diagnosis. This is the "CSA five" from
your Exam script, made teachable:

**1 — Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS).** Subjective daytime sleepiness. 8
everyday situations, each rated 0–3 (chance of dozing). Total **0–24**; **≥10–11**
suggests excessive daytime sleepiness.

**2 — STOP-BANG.** Risk stratification, 8 yes/no items:
**S**noring loud · **T**iredness · **O**bserved to stop breathing · **P**ressure
(treated high BP) · **B**MI > 35 · **A**ge > 50 · **N**eck circumference large
(~>16" / 40cm) · **G**ender male. Score: **0–2** low · **3–4** intermediate ·
**5–8** high risk.

**3 — Mallampati (modified).** Visual airway crowding, patient seated, mouth open:
**Class I** (soft palate, uvula, pillars visible) → **Class IV** (only hard
palate visible). Higher class = more crowding = higher airway risk.

**4 — Oral/structural signs.** Tongue **scalloping** (crenations from a tongue
crowded against the teeth), mouth-breathing signs, high-arched/narrow palate,
tonsil size.

**5 — Cranial-postural screen.** Forward head posture, cranial/facial asymmetry,
cervical position — your structural risk read.

**Home tracking to add:** SnoreLab or a sleep app, and pulse-oximetry for
overnight oxygen — the bridge from screening to the physician-read HST.

## Key terms (quick glossary)

- **AHI / RDI / ODI** — event and oxygen-dip indices from a sleep study.
- **Hypopnea** — partial airflow reduction with desaturation or arousal.
- **UARS** — increased airway resistance and arousals without frank apneas.
- **Genioglossus** — main tongue-protruding / airway-dilating muscle.
- **Retrognathia** — a set-back lower jaw; crowds the airway.
- **Macroglossia** — enlarged tongue.
- **Mallampati** — visual airway-crowding class (I–IV).
- **FHP** — forward head posture; reduces airway space.
