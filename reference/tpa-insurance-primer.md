## ⚠️ CRITICAL — Risk note for Dr. Z (save this especially)

> When a patient's employer "provides" a supplement to their CA EDD SDI, the
> **employer (or the employer's carrier) is the one on the hook for the money —
> Sedgwick just processes it.**

**Why this is a risk to Dr. Z:** If Dr. Z ever delivers care expecting a
patient's "insurance" or SDI-supplement to pay, the money is **not** coming
from an insurance company in the classic sense. It's coming from the employer
(if self-funded) or the employer's carrier. **Sedgwick is only the processor /
gatekeeper** — a Third-Party Administrator that can delay, question, or deny,
but never actually owns the money. Never assume "there's insurance" means
"someone will pay." Always know **who actually holds the money** and **who
decides**. This is the single biggest reason CSA's cash-pay model is cleaner.

**Save this too — the verb "hire":** *Employers and carriers **hire** TPAs* to
handle their **disability leave, workers' comp, and FMLA claims.** The TPA works
for the payer, not the patient.

## The teaching (Q&A)

### Who is "whoever" / who pays the TPA?
The **payer** — whoever bears the financial risk and hires the TPA. That's an
**employer** (if self-funded), an **insurance carrier**, or a government program.
They pay the TPA an **administrative fee** (per-claim, per-member-per-month, or
flat). The patient/claimant does **not** pay the TPA.

### Who is a carrier?
The **insurance carrier** = the actual insurance company that underwrites the
policy and **carries the risk** — it pays claims out of its own reserves
(Aetna, BCBS, Cigna, UHC, MetLife for disability, etc.). "Carrier" literally =
the one who *carries* the risk. **Opposite of a TPA**, which carries none.

### What is an Administrative Services Agreement (ASA)?
The contract where the risk-bearer (a plan or an insurer) **hires** the
administrator (TPA) and spells out: which admin duties the TPA performs, how it's
paid, the standards it must meet, how it handles money/records/data, and who's
liable. It's the document that turns a company into "the TPA **for** this
specific plan." Also called an **ASO** (Administrative Services Only) agreement.

### Can the TPA's business layer be a sole proprietor?
Technically some states (incl. CA) allow an **individual** to register as an
administrator — but **don't.** A sole prop puts your personal assets on the line
for claims-handling errors, and you'll struggle to get bonded and taken
seriously. Use an **LLC or corporation** for liability protection, bonding, and
credibility.

### What is a plan / a sponsor / a plan sponsor?
- **Plan** = a formal benefit arrangement — a defined set of benefits (health,
  disability, retirement) with written rules on who's eligible and what's
  covered. In federal law, an "employee benefit plan."
- **Sponsor** = the entity that establishes and maintains it.
- **Plan sponsor** = usually the **employer** (or union/association) that creates
  and funds the plan. **If self-funded, the plan sponsor IS the risk-bearer** —
  that's the "my insurance is really myself" idea. (CA SDI is a *state program*,
  not a plan sponsor; the employer offering the SDI *supplement* is the sponsor.)

### How many states require a TPA license?
The **large majority — on the order of 40-plus states — require a license or
registration** to act as a TPA; a **handful** have no distinct TPA license and
regulate the activity another way. It is **not** a clean 50/0 split and the exact
list shifts — the **SPBA TPA Licensing Survey** is the authority to check
state-by-state before operating anywhere specific.

### Registering — where / what / why?
- **Where:** the state's **Department of Insurance** (for life/health/disability
  claims), in **each state** you operate. In CA that's the CDI. (Workers' comp is
  a different door — see below.)
- **What:** your **business entity as an "administrator"** — you file the
  application (CA: form **LIC 441-12**), disclose owners/officers, financials, the
  bond, and the lines you'll administer.
- **Why:** because you're touching **other people's premium money and claims
  decisions.** Registration is the state vetting your solvency, competence, and
  honesty — and keeping audit/enforcement power over you. No registration =
  acting illegally as an unlicensed administrator.

### "Getting burned" — who does the burning?
Not Salem, no pitchforks — but the modern equivalent: **regulatory
enforcement.** The **same regulator who licenses you (state DOI, or DIR for
workers' comp) is the one who can fine you, issue a cease-and-desist, and
suspend or revoke your certificate.** Add the **federal DOL** for ERISA plans and
**civil lawsuits** from plans/claimants. The Commissioner giveth and the
Commissioner taketh away.

### Surety bond, E&O, fidelity coverage
- **Surety bond:** a three-party financial guarantee (you, the state, a surety
  company) that you'll perform lawfully. If you mishandle funds, a claim is paid
  from the bond and **you must repay the surety.** Protects the public/plans —
  **not you.**
- **E&O (Errors & Omissions):** professional liability insurance covering
  financial harm from your **mistakes/negligence** (a botched claim, a wrong
  eligibility call).
- **Fidelity bond/coverage:** protects against **employee dishonesty/theft**
  (e.g., an employee embezzling plan funds). ERISA **requires** fidelity bonding
  for anyone handling plan assets.

### ERISA / DOL
- **ERISA** = Employee Retirement Income Security Act of 1974 — the federal law
  governing **private-sector** employee benefit plans (retirement **and**
  health/welfare).
- **DOL** = U.S. Department of Labor; its **EBSA** division enforces ERISA.
- **For a TPA:** when it administers a **self-funded employer health plan**, that
  plan is governed by **ERISA (federal)**, which preempts much state insurance
  law. The TPA must follow ERISA rules on claims procedures, appeals,
  disclosures, fiduciary duties, and fidelity bonding. So a health-plan TPA lives
  under **both** state licensing **and** federal ERISA. (Government plans, church
  plans, and state programs like **CA SDI are generally NOT ERISA.**)

### Is there a separate TPA track for workers' comp? — Yes.
WC claims administration is a **distinct regulatory track.** One company can do
both, but WC administration for self-insured employers is authorized separately.

### The two CA tracks, differentiated
| | **Track A — Life/Health/Disability** | **Track B — Workers' Comp** |
|---|---|---|
| Regulator | **CA Department of Insurance (CDI)** | **CA Dept. of Industrial Relations (DIR)**, Office of Self-Insurance Plans |
| Credential | **Certificate of Registration as Administrator** (§1759, form LIC 441-12, ~$188 / 2 yrs) | **Certificate of Consent to Administer** |
| Covers | sickness, disability, health, annuities, premiums/claims | on-the-job injury claims for self-insured employers |
| Trigger | you touch insured **health/disability** money | you touch **workers' comp** money |

### "If I administer insured claims, I'm in the insurance regulator's house." — Am I? As Dr. Z? As CSA?
- **As Dr. Z, the treating chiropractor** billing/collecting for **your own
  services** (cash, or even insurance for your own care): **NO.** Providing care
  and billing for it ≠ administering someone else's insurance. You're a
  **provider**, governed by your chiropractic board and normal billing law.
- **As CSA, IF it ever adjusted/settled/collected premiums on *other parties'*
  insured claims: YES** — that pulls CSA into the DOI's house as an administrator.
- **The line:** administering **your own** care revenue = provider (fine).
  Administering **others'** insured claims/premiums = TPA = regulated. **As long
  as CSA stays cash-pay for its own pathway, it is not a TPA and not in that
  house.**

### NAIC "Registration and Regulation of Third-Party Administrators" model act
- **NAIC** = National Association of Insurance Commissioners — the coordinating
  body of all 50 state insurance regulators. It has **no direct authority
  itself**; it publishes **model laws** states can adopt to stay roughly
  consistent.
- The TPA **model act** is the template most states used to build their TPA
  licensing laws: who counts as a TPA, the required written agreement, bonding,
  recordkeeping, fiduciary handling of funds, reporting, and grounds for
  discipline. It's **why** TPA rules look similar state to state — but each state
  tweaks it, so you still verify state-by-state.

### What is an insured pool?
A **group of people/risks combined so the premiums of the many fund the claims of
the unlucky few.** That's how insurance works — pooling. A **self-insured /
self-funded pool** is when an employer or association funds its **own** pool
instead of buying a carrier's. **The TPA administers the pool; it doesn't own
it.** CSA cash-pay = **no pool.** To use a TPA, you'd first need a pool (members
funding a shared benefit) for the TPA to run.

## 📇 INDEX / GLOSSARY (the "what is X" terms, alphabetized)

- **Administrative Services Agreement (ASA / ASO):** contract hiring a TPA to run
  a specific plan's admin.
- **Carrier:** the insurance company that underwrites and carries the risk; pays
  claims from its own reserves.
- **Certificate of Consent to Administer:** CA DIR credential for a workers'-comp
  administrator.
- **Certificate of Registration as Administrator:** CA DOI credential (§1759) for
  a life/health/disability administrator.
- **DOL (Dept. of Labor) / EBSA:** federal enforcer of ERISA.
- **E&O (Errors & Omissions):** insurance covering financial harm from your
  professional mistakes.
- **ERISA:** 1974 federal law governing private-sector employee benefit plans.
- **Fidelity bond/coverage:** protection against employee theft of plan funds
  (ERISA-required).
- **Insured pool:** the group whose combined premiums fund the few who have
  claims.
- **NAIC:** the 50-state insurance-commissioner coordinating body; writes model
  laws, holds no direct power.
- **Payer / "whoever":** the risk-bearer who hires and pays the TPA (employer,
  carrier, or gov program).
- **Plan:** a formal, written benefit arrangement for a group.
- **Plan sponsor:** the entity (usually the employer) that establishes and funds
  the plan; if self-funded, it IS the risk-bearer.
- **Sedgwick:** a large **TPA** — processes disability/leave/WC/FMLA claims for
  employers & carriers; carries no risk.
- **Sole proprietor (as TPA):** technically possible in some states, practically
  unwise (personal-asset exposure).
- **Sponsor:** the entity that establishes and maintains a plan.
- **Surety bond:** three-party guarantee you'll perform lawfully; protects the
  public, not you.
- **TPA (Third-Party Administrator):** a hired administrator that processes/
  manages claims & benefits for a payer, **carrying no insurance risk.**

## Strategic read (brand-guardian note)

**CSA becoming a TPA is almost certainly NOT the move** — CSA's pathway is
deliberately cash-pay, so there's no insured pool to administer, and a TPA is a
separately-regulated, bonded, audited business with its own liability. **Parked
idea, not a July/August goal.**

**The useful nugget:** the smarter version is *CSA designs a membership/plan and
hires an existing TPA to administer it* (see the membership design note) — you
rent the admin brain, you don't become the regulated one. And this literacy
directly sharpens the **open HST-vendor / insurance-billing decision.**

**Adjacent live idea (Shirazi bottleneck):** insurance **benefit-verification**
(eligibility + patient-responsibility quote) is normal revenue-cycle work and
does **NOT** require a TPA license — as long as you verify benefits and collect
for a provider's **own** services, not settle a payer's claims. That keeps the
"one-page pre-verified cost sheet" idea license-light. See goals/parked ideas.
