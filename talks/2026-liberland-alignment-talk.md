# 1. CLOSE YOUR MOUTH (4:00)
### Open the gate.

**Line:** *"Your mouth is for eating and talking. Your nose is for breathing.
Most people have those two jobs mixed up."*

*↩ Optional, only if it landed last time:* "Last time I read you the line from
Genesis — breathed into his **nostrils.** Not the mouth. That was the original
design." One line. Do not preach it twice.

**Why it matters (about 90 seconds):**
- The nose warms the air, cleans it, and adds water to it. The mouth does none
  of that. Air goes down your throat cold, dry, and dirty.
- The nose is also the **first gate of the airway** — and it is meant to be
  tight. That resistance is not a fault. It slows the air down and makes your
  lungs work properly. Breathing through your mouth is like taking the door off
  the house because the door was slowing you down.
- And the nose does one more thing. It puts your **tongue up on the roof of
  your mouth** — where it belongs. That tongue position helps hold the whole
  airway open behind it. Mouth open, tongue drops back. Tongue back, airway
  narrows.

**How to know it is you (about 45 seconds):**
- Dry mouth in the morning.
- Sore or scratchy throat when you wake up.
- Waking up thirsty in the night.
- Drool on the pillow.
- And the easiest one — if someone sleeps next to you, just ask them.

**What to do tonight (about 90 seconds):**
- **Practice in the daytime first.** Walk with your mouth closed. Thirty or
  forty minutes. If you cannot hold nose breathing on a slow walk, you will
  not hold it asleep.
- If your nose feels too small for the job, that is usually because you have
  not used it in years. It gets easier in about two weeks.
- **The warning, say it out loud:** do not tape your mouth shut at night
  because you saw it online. Not until you know whether you have untreated
  sleep apnea. Taping over a real problem is covering the alarm, not putting
  out the fire. Point three tells you how to find out.

**Land it:**
> That is the gate. It is free, it starts tonight, and you have been walking
> around with it open.

# 2. LIFT YOUR HEAD (4:00)
### Straighten the pipe.

**Line:** *"Your head weighs about five kilos. Every centimeter it drifts
forward, your neck carries it like it weighs more."*

**Why it matters (about 2 minutes):**
- Here is the thing almost nobody knows. **The airway is not just a tube.** It
  is held open — or squeezed shut — by everything around it. Bone. Soft tissue.
  Your tongue. Your jaw. The position of your neck. Even how wound up your
  nervous system is.
- The bones of your face are not decoration. They are **the architecture your
  breath moves through.**
- And it does not start at the neck. It runs the whole way up: feet, hips,
  spine, ribs, neck, jaw, palate, skull. Change the bottom and the top follows.
  Change the top and you feel it all the way down.
- Now put a phone in that system. Head goes forward. Neck bends. The space at
  the front of your throat gets smaller. **Your posture and your breathing are
  the same subject.** I cannot separate them and neither can you.

*↩ Say it plain:* "This is the kink I showed you last time. And it is the one
you are doing to yourself right now, looking down at a phone."

**What to do (about 90 seconds):**
- **Screen to eye level.** Phone off your lap. This is the single biggest one
  and it costs nothing.
- **Walk every day.** Thirty or forty minutes. Your spine has no pump — the
  discs feed themselves by moving, pressure on, pressure off. Sit still all day
  and you switch that off. I am not going to tell you to stand up every thirty
  minutes. Nobody does that in real life. Walk.
- **Come down before bed.** A wound-up body does not go into deep sleep. You
  lie down but the engine keeps running. Slow breath through the nose, and make
  the breath **out** longer than the breath in. Five or ten minutes. Same
  routine every night — the routine itself becomes the signal.
- **Notice your jaw.** Clicking, morning tightness, worn flat teeth, headaches
  at the temples. The jaw sits right on top of the airway, and where your lower
  jaw sits decides how much room your tongue has behind it. If you are grinding
  at night, that is worth taking seriously, not waiting out.

**Land it:**
> Bodies take the shape you hold most. That is not a character flaw. That is how
> tissue works. But it does mean the shape you hold most is a choice — even
> when it does not feel like you are choosing.

# 3. FIND OUT (4:00)
### Measure it.

**Line:** *"Snoring is not a personality. It is the sound of tissue shaking in a
narrow airway. It is information."*

**The signs — say them slowly, people are checking themselves (about 1 minute):**
- Loud snoring.
- Someone has watched you stop breathing.
- You gasp or choke awake.
- You wake up tired after a full night in bed.
- Headaches in the morning.
- Falling asleep in the daytime.
- Blood pressure that will not come down.

> If two or three of those are you, you do not have a sleep habit problem. You
> have a question that needs an answer.

**The cheap version — start tonight (about 1 minute):**
- A ring, a wrist band, or a phone app. Any of them. Doing it **every night**
  matters more than the brand.
- Watch the trend, not one night. One bad night means nothing. Two months of
  shallow sleep means something.
- People say "I sleep fine." Then they measure, and find they wake eleven times
  a night and remember none of it.
- Be fair to the device. It is not a medical test. It is a mirror. It tells you
  whether to look closer.

**The real version (about 1 minute):**
- A **home sleep test.** A small device comes to your house. You wear it one
  night, in your own bed. A doctor reads it.
- **I do not read it and I do not diagnose it.** A medical doctor does. I read
  the structure.
- If it comes back positive, there are treatments that work — CPAP, mouth
  devices, sometimes surgery. They exist because they help the right people.
  I am not here to talk anybody out of any of them.

*↩ The disarm, short:* "Same as last time. I am not throwing rocks at the mask.
I do not have to." Six seconds. Buys the whole talk.

**Why not wait (about 1 minute):**
- Pain and tiredness are **late** signals. Bodies cover. They borrow from one
  place to hide a loss in another, and they do it quietly. "I feel fine" is not
  proof. It means the cover-up is still holding.

*↩ Your line from last time, and this is where it belongs:*
> The system waits until you qualify. A symptom. A diagnosis. A label. I start
> before you are anyone's patient.

*Last time that was an idea. Tonight it is the instruction.*

**Land it:**
> Get a picture while you feel good. Then look again later. A first measurement
> you never compare to is just a souvenir.

## Q&A — 10 minutes (17:00 – 27:00)

A third of your time on stage. Run it, do not let it run you.

**How to start it.** Rooms freeze for the first ten seconds. Ask your own
question first:

> The one I always get first is — how do I know if I breathe through my mouth
> at night, when I am asleep for the whole thing? Dry mouth. Sore throat.
> Waking up thirsty. Drool on the pillow. And if someone sleeps next to you,
> ask them. Now — yours.

That fills the silence and shows them the size of answer you want.

### No mic. People shout. Six rules.

1. **Repeat every question out loud. Every single one.** Half the room did not
   hear it, and if anyone is recording, the recording only has your voice. It
   also buys you two seconds to think.
2. **Take one at a time, out loud.** Three hands up, name the order: "You, then
   you in the blue, then the back." Now nobody talks over anybody.
3. **Walk toward whoever is asking.** You will hear them and the room will
   follow you and go quiet. Standing still and squinting makes it louder.
4. **Never guess at a question you did not hear.** "Say that again, louder."
   Twice if you have to. Answering the wrong question burns a minute and looks
   like you were not listening.
5. **Go get the back of the room.** In a shout format only the front rows ever
   ask. Look up: "Somebody in the back." Then wait. It feels long. It works.
6. **Answer in about 45 seconds.** Ten minutes is roughly twelve questions
   tight, or five if you ramble.

**If one person will not stop:** "Let me take a couple more and I will find you
after." Warm — then **turn your body** to the other side of the room. Turning
ends it. Words alone do not.

**At 26:00, say "last question."** Then hold the floor.

### Never say
- That chiropractic, or VAET, or any structural care cures or fixes sleep apnea.
- That anyone can stop using CPAP or a mouth device. Not your call.
- Anything against sleep doctors, dentists, or ENTs. You **add** a layer. You do
  not replace anybody.
- Any promise about a person you have not examined.

### Ready answers

*"Can you fix my sleep apnea?"*
> No. I do not diagnose it and I do not claim to cure it. I work on the
> structure around the airway. Your first step is a real test read by a doctor.
> Then we talk about structure.

*"Is CPAP bad?"*
> No. CPAP works, and for many people it is the right answer. The honest problem
> is that some people cannot stick with it. That is a real gap worth talking
> about. It is not an argument against the machine.

*"What about taping my mouth?"*
> Nose breathing at night is a good goal. Taping before you know whether you
> have untreated apnea is not how I would get there. Find out first.

*"My nose is always blocked. I cannot breathe through it."*
> Then that is your starting point, and it is worth having someone look at it.
> Noses do take turns through the day — that part is normal. Always blocked on
> one side is not.

*"Which ring or band should I buy?"*
> Any of them. The one you will actually wear every night. You are watching a
> trend, not taking a medical reading.

*"What numbers should I watch?"*
> How long you slept, how deep it went, how many times you woke, and your
> resting heart rate. Over weeks, not nights.

*"How many hours should I sleep?"*
> Enough that you wake without an alarm and get through the afternoon without
> crashing. That number is not the same for you and the person beside you. And
> depth beats length — nine broken hours is worse than seven solid ones. Going
> to bed and waking at the same time every day does more than chasing a number.

*"What about morning light?"*
> Good question and it is cheap. Get outside in the morning, ten or fifteen
> minutes, even on a grey day. Dim things down at night. Most people who cannot
> fall asleep do not have a broken clock — they have a late one.

*"I grind my teeth."*
> Take it seriously rather than waiting it out. Grinding often travels with poor
> sleep and poor breathing — the body working while it should be resting. See a
> dentist who thinks about the airway, and get the structure looked at.

*"I wake at three every morning."*
> Common, and usually a nervous system still running. Look at your wind-down,
> and at what your tracker shows for that part of the night. If it comes with
> snoring or gasping, get tested.

*"I do breathwork already. Wim Hof, Buteyko."*
> Good, and I mean that — those men taught millions how to breathe better, me
> included. But technique moves air through the pipe. It does not straighten
> the pipe. Do both.

*"Where do I get tested?"*
> Talk to me after. In most places the device comes to your house, you sleep in
> your own bed, and a doctor reads it.

*"What do you actually do with your hands?"*
> I work top down — skull, nose, jaw, neck, posture. By hand. The goal is more
> room and more motion in the structure your breath travels through.
