> ## Scope statement — read to every patient, recorded on every form
>
> This is a **structural** evaluation. As a chiropractor I do **not** diagnose
> obstructive sleep apnea or any sleep disorder. Diagnosis comes from a
> physician reading a sleep study. My job is to examine the structure around
> your airway — skull, jaw, neck, and posture — to screen for signs that
> warrant objective testing, to educate you, to refer when appropriate, and to
> deliver structural care.
>
> Structural airway care, including VAET, is **not** a cure for sleep apnea and
> is **not** a replacement for CPAP, an oral appliance, surgery, or any care
> your physician or dentist has prescribed. It is one layer of a
> multidisciplinary picture.

## Section E — The seven jaw-and-posture signs

**These are the specific screening signs the patient-facing "How Your Jaw
Affects Your Posture" education piece covers.** Every one gets tested, scored,
and photographed where noted. Each entry below states the test, the record, and
the honest structural reasoning — say it to the patient the same way.

---

### E1. Jaw clenching / bruxism

**How to test**
- Palpate **masseter** and **temporalis** at rest and on clench — note bulk,
  tone, and tenderness bilaterally.
- Look for the corroborating intraoral signs from D5: wear facets, linea alba,
  tongue scalloping, abfractions.
- Ask about morning jaw soreness, morning headache in the temples, and bed
  partner report of grinding sounds.

| Item | Right | Left |
|---|---|---|
| Masseter bulk | ☐ Normal ☐ Hypertrophic | ☐ Normal ☐ Hypertrophic |
| Masseter tenderness (0–10) | ______ | ______ |
| Temporalis tenderness (0–10) | ______ | ______ |
| Medial pterygoid tenderness (0–10) | ______ | ______ |

- Daytime clenching awareness: ☐ None ☐ Occasional ☐ Frequent
- Nighttime grinding reported by partner: ☐ Yes ☐ No ☐ Unknown ☐ No partner
- Night guard in use: ☐ Yes ☐ No — helping? ☐ Yes ☐ No ☐ Partially

**Overall clenching/bruxism impression:** ☐ None ☐ Suspected ☐ Probable ☐ Marked

**What we say about it**
Sustained clenching means sustained load through the masticatory system, and
those muscles do not work in isolation from the neck. Elevated jaw-muscle
activity and cervical muscle activity travel together, so persistent clenching
belongs in a structural exam of the head and neck. Grinding is also frequently
observed alongside disrupted breathing during sleep — which is one more reason
to get objective sleep data rather than guess. **We do not tell a patient their
grinding is caused by apnea, and we do not tell them adjusting will stop the
grinding.**

**Refer out if:** significant dental damage, cracked teeth, or a guard is
needed → DDS.

---

### E2. Forward head posture (FHP)

**How to test — measure it, don't eyeball it**
- **Wall-occiput test:** heels, buttocks, and upper back to the wall, chin
  level. Measure the gap from wall to occiput. **Normal = touches (0 cm).**
- **Tragus-to-wall** distance: ______ cm
- **Craniovertebral angle** measured from the right lateral posture photo
  (angle between the horizontal and the line from C7 spinous process to the
  tragus). **Larger angle = better alignment; roughly < 50° is commonly
  treated as forward head posture.**

| Measure | Value |
|---|---|
| Wall–occiput gap | ______ cm |
| Tragus-to-wall | ______ cm |
| Craniovertebral angle (from photo) | ______ ° |
| Tragus anterior to acromion (lateral photo, horizontal offset) | ______ cm |
| Shoulder protraction / rounded shoulders | ☐ None ☐ Mild ☐ Moderate ☐ Marked |
| Thoracic kyphosis | ☐ Normal ☐ Increased |

**Grade:** ☐ None ☐ Mild ☐ Moderate ☐ Marked

**What we say about it**
Head position changes the geometry of the upper airway — carrying the head
forward alters the space behind the tongue and changes how the muscles of the
neck and jaw have to work. It also loads the cervical spine and changes how the
rib cage moves, which changes the mechanics of breathing. That is the
structural argument, and it is enough. **Do not claim a specific percentage of
lost lung capacity or oxygen** — that number gets repeated on the internet and
it is not something this exam measures.

### E3. Uneven bite pressure

**How to test**
- Have the patient tap into their bite several times and report **where they
  feel first and hardest contact.**
- Palpate masseters bilaterally **during** clench and compare tone and timing.
- Check dental midline vs. facial midline (D5).
- Note any lateral shift of the mandible on closing.
- Articulating paper, if used in-office: mark and record. Otherwise note that
  the objective occlusal analysis belongs to the DDS.

| Item | Finding |
|---|---|
| Patient-reported first/heaviest contact | ☐ Even ☐ Right ☐ Left ☐ Anterior ☐ Posterior |
| Masseter activation symmetry on clench | ☐ Symmetric ☐ R dominant ☐ L dominant |
| Mandibular shift on closure | ☐ None ☐ ____ mm to ☐ L ☐ R |
| Occlusal wear pattern symmetry | ☐ Symmetric ☐ Asymmetric — heavier: ☐ L ☐ R |
| Chewing-side preference | ☐ None ☐ R ☐ L |

**What we say about it**
An asymmetric bite means asymmetric muscle work, and asymmetric muscle work
through the head and neck shows up in the postural exam. We document the
asymmetry, we correlate it with what we see in the photos, and **occlusion
itself is the dentist's call** — we coordinate, we do not adjust the bite.

**Refer out if:** notable occlusal asymmetry, a bite that changed recently, or
an appliance that may be shifting it → DDS.

### E4. Vertical opening — the "three fingers" test

**How to test — the finger test is the field version; millimeters are the record**
- Patient stacks index, middle, and ring fingers vertically between the
  incisors. **Passing = three fingers fit.**
- Then **measure maximal interincisal opening (MIO)** with a ruler or Boley
  gauge. Typical adult range is roughly **40–55 mm**; **under about 40 mm is
  generally considered restricted.** Add the overbite to the interincisal gap
  for a true MIO.

| Measure | Value |
|---|---|
| Three-finger test | ☐ Passes ☐ Two fingers only ☐ One finger |
| **Maximal interincisal opening** | ______ mm |
| Overbite added | ______ mm  → **Corrected MIO ______ mm** |
| Pain limits opening? | ☐ No ☐ Yes — where: __________ |
| Lateral excursion R ______ mm · L ______ mm | (typical ≈ 8–12 mm each) |
| Protrusion | ______ mm (typical ≈ 6–9 mm) |
| Opening path | ☐ Straight ☐ Deviates and corrects (☐ L ☐ R) ☐ Deflects and stays (☐ L ☐ R) |

**What we say about it**
Limited opening is limited joint and muscle motion. It tells us the
masticatory system is guarding, and a guarded jaw sits on top of a guarded
neck. **Deflection that does not correct** is a different finding than
deviation that corrects — document which one you see, because it changes who
else needs to look at this patient.

**Refer out if:** locking, sudden loss of opening, or a deflection that does
not correct → DDS/TMD specialist before structural care escalates.

### E7. Chronic mouth breathing

**How to test — observe before you announce you are observing**
- Watch **lip seal at rest** during history-taking, before the patient knows
  it is being assessed.
- **Lip incompetence:** do the lips rest apart at neutral?
- **Tongue rest posture:** is the tongue on the palate or on the floor of the
  mouth?
- **Nasal-only breathing trial:** 60 seconds, lips sealed. Note effort.
- **BOLT / comfortable breath hold** from B3.
- Look for the associated signs: dry mouth, dry/chapped lips, gingival dryness,
  long lower facial third, allergic shiners, a narrow high palate.

**What we say about it**
Nasal breathing supports tongue-to-palate posture; mouth breathing lets the
tongue drop and the head drift forward to keep the airway open. That is a
structural chain we can see, measure, and photograph. Chronic mouth breathing
is also a reason to make sure the **nose** has been properly evaluated —
if the nasal airway is obstructed, that is an ENT question, not a chiropractic
one.

**Refer out if:** persistent nasal obstruction, suspected septal deviation,
polyps, or chronic sinusitis → ENT.
