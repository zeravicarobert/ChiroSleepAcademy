Scope statement — read to every patient, recorded on every form

This is a structural evaluation. As a chiropractor I do not diagnose obstructive sleep apnea or any sleep disorder. Diagnosis comes from a physician reading a sleep study. My job is to examine the structure around your airway — skull, jaw, neck, and posture — to screen for signs that warrant objective testing, to educate you, to refer when appropriate, and to deliver structural care.

Structural airway care, including VAET, is not a cure for sleep apnea and is not a replacement for CPAP, an oral appliance, surgery, or any care your physician or dentist has prescribed. It is one layer of a multidisciplinary picture.

Section E — The seven jaw-and-posture signs

These are the specific screening signs the patient-facing "How Your Jaw Affects Your Posture" education piece covers. Every one gets tested, scored, and photographed where noted. Each entry below states the test, the record, and the honest structural reasoning — say it to the patient the same way.

E1. Jaw clenching / bruxism

How to test - Palpate masseter and temporalis at rest and on clench — note bulk, tone, and tenderness bilaterally. - Look for the corroborating intraoral signs from D5: wear facets, linea alba, tongue scalloping, abfractions. - Ask about morning jaw soreness, morning headache in the temples, and bed partner report of grinding sounds.

  • Night guard in use: Yes No — helping? Yes No Partially

What we say about it Sustained clenching means sustained load through the masticatory system, and those muscles do not work in isolation from the neck. Elevated jaw-muscle activity and cervical muscle activity travel together, so persistent clenching belongs in a structural exam of the head and neck. Grinding is also frequently observed alongside disrupted breathing during sleep — which is one more reason to get objective sleep data rather than guess. We do not tell a patient their grinding is caused by apnea, and we do not tell them adjusting will stop the grinding.

Refer out if: significant dental damage, cracked teeth, or a guard is needed → DDS.

E2. Forward head posture (FHP)

How to test — measure it, don't eyeball it - Wall-occiput test: heels, buttocks, and upper back to the wall, chin level. Measure the gap from wall to occiput. Normal = touches (0 cm). - Tragus-to-wall distance: cm - Craniovertebral angle measured from the right lateral posture photo (angle between the horizontal and the line from C7 spinous process to the tragus). Larger angle = better alignment; roughly < 50° is commonly treated as forward head posture.

What we say about it Head position changes the geometry of the upper airway — carrying the head forward alters the space behind the tongue and changes how the muscles of the neck and jaw have to work. It also loads the cervical spine and changes how the rib cage moves, which changes the mechanics of breathing. That is the structural argument, and it is enough. Do not claim a specific percentage of lost lung capacity or oxygen — that number gets repeated on the internet and it is not something this exam measures.

E3. Uneven bite pressure

How to test - Have the patient tap into their bite several times and report where they feel first and hardest contact. - Palpate masseters bilaterally during clench and compare tone and timing. - Check dental midline vs. facial midline (D5). - Note any lateral shift of the mandible on closing. - Articulating paper, if used in-office: mark and record. Otherwise note that the objective occlusal analysis belongs to the DDS.

What we say about it An asymmetric bite means asymmetric muscle work, and asymmetric muscle work through the head and neck shows up in the postural exam. We document the asymmetry, we correlate it with what we see in the photos, and occlusion itself is the dentist's call — we coordinate, we do not adjust the bite.

Refer out if: notable occlusal asymmetry, a bite that changed recently, or an appliance that may be shifting it → DDS.

E4. Vertical opening — the "three fingers" test

How to test — the finger test is the field version; millimeters are the record - Patient stacks index, middle, and ring fingers vertically between the incisors. Passing = three fingers fit. - Then measure maximal interincisal opening (MIO) with a ruler or Boley gauge. Typical adult range is roughly 40–55 mm; under about 40 mm is generally considered restricted. Add the overbite to the interincisal gap for a true MIO.

What we say about it Limited opening is limited joint and muscle motion. It tells us the masticatory system is guarding, and a guarded jaw sits on top of a guarded neck. Deflection that does not correct is a different finding than deviation that corrects — document which one you see, because it changes who else needs to look at this patient.

Refer out if: locking, sudden loss of opening, or a deflection that does not correct → DDS/TMD specialist before structural care escalates.

What we say about it The TMJ does not move independently of the cervical spine — jaw position and head position influence each other, and pain in one region commonly presents alongside dysfunction in the other. What we say about it Nasal breathing supports tongue-to-palate posture; mouth breathing lets the tongue drop and the head drift forward to keep the airway open. Chronic mouth breathing is also a reason to make sure the nose has been properly evaluated — if the nasal airway is obstructed, that is an ENT question, not a chiropractic one.

Refer out if: persistent nasal obstruction, suspected septal deviation, polyps, or chronic sinusitis → ENT.
